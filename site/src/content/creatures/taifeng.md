## The Mountain of Nine Rivers

Mount He (和山) is not just another peak in the Shanhaijing's long catalogue. It is the nine convergences of the Yellow River — the place where nine tributary rivers join and begin the flow that defines Chinese civilization's heartland. The *Classic of the Mountains: Central, Part IX* describes the mountain's top as bare stone (jade and bi-stone, but no grass or trees), its fauna as entirely white (white wolves, white tigers, white pheasants, white peacocks), and its governance as Taifeng's domain.

No grass or trees. White animals. A deity that glows. The mountain is already luminous before Taifeng arrives; the fauna have been bleached by proximity to the divine. Taifeng is the apex of this existing radiance — a guardian whose light is not external (a torch, a lamp) but intrinsic, a visible expression of its nature.

## Tiger Tail, Human Form

The tiger tail is the Shanhaijing's most common marker for fierce protective authority. Deities and creatures with tiger tails appear across the text as guardians, threshold-keepers, and divine enforcers. Taifeng's tail is the emblem of its willingness to be dangerous — it guards a sacred location, and it has the anatomy required to enforce that guardianship.

But Taifeng is more than a guardian with teeth. The light sets it apart. Most Shanhaijing deities with protective functions express their power through size, claws, or multiple heads. Taifeng's power is expressed as radiance. "Entering and leaving with light" is the same construction used elsewhere in the text for creatures whose movement reveals their divine nature — the light is not cast outward intentionally, it simply accompanies the deity as an inherent quality.

## The Ten Luminances

The phrase 十辉之地 — "the land of the ten luminances" — appears only here in the Shanhaijing and has been variously interpreted. Guo Pu, the fourth-century commentator, suggests a connection to the ten suns of the pre-Yi mythology (the era when ten suns rose simultaneously and Hou Yi shot down nine). Others read it as a reference to ten celestial phenomena that converge at this geographic node — astronomical lights, divine radiances, or the combined luminescence of the nine rivers and the guardian deity.

Whatever the precise reference, Taifeng is the deity of luminous governance: it does not merely guard Mount He, it *oversees* the land of lights. The role is custodial and celestial — a guardian whose domain is both geographic (a mountain) and luminous (a field of radiance).
