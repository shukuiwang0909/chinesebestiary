## The Commission

The Shanhaijing records it briefly: Di Jun, the supreme deity of heaven, gave Yi a red bow and white-fletched arrows and sent him down to the lower world to "relieve the ten thousand hardships of the earth."

This is the foundational moment of Hou Yi's myth — not the sun-shooting, which comes later, but the moment of commissioning. He is given his weapons by the father of the ten suns he will eventually shoot. Di Jun knew what he was sending Yi to do. Whether he understood what it would cost both of them is a question the myth leaves open.

The name "Hou Yi" means roughly "Lord Archer Yi." The Shanhaijing calls him simply "Yi" (羿). The honorific "Hou" — a noble title — was added later, reflecting centuries of veneration. By the time the Han dynasty compiled the great mythological encyclopedias, Yi had become the model of the divine hero: the one who did the necessary thing and paid the full price.

## Ten Suns

The crisis that made Yi's mission necessary was this: in the time of the sage-king Yao, ten suns appeared in the sky at once.

Xi He, wife of Di Jun, was the mother of the ten suns. Each morning she would drive one of her son-suns across the sky in a dragon chariot; at dusk she would bathe it in the Sweet Abyss at the world's edge. The ten suns were meant to take turns. On the day they all appeared together, no one knows whether it was rebellion, accident, or joy that drove them. The effect was catastrophic: the crops burned, the rivers dried, the forests caught fire, and the people had nothing to eat.

Yi received Di Jun's bow and went up to the sun's path. He shot nine of the ten suns out of the sky.

The *Huainanzi* adds a detail that every later reader has cherished: Yao, watching Yi work, grew worried that he would shoot them all. He quietly removed one of Yi's arrows from his quiver. Yi reached for it, found it gone, and stopped at nine. One sun remained. The world cooled. The rivers refilled. The harvest came.

## The Campaign

The suns were not the only catastrophe. The *Huainanzi* lists six monsters Yi destroyed in the same campaign:

**Ya Yu** (猰貐) — a creature with a human face and the body of a horse or dragon that devoured people; killed south of the sun's path.

**Zao Chi** (凿齿) — a monster with enormous protruding tusks that could tear apart anything; executed in the wilds of Chouhua.

**Jiu Ying** (九婴) — a nine-headed water and fire beast that made the floods and flames uncontrollable; killed at Xiong Water.

**Da Feng** (大风) — a great wind that could overturn mountains and destroy cities; shot down at the marsh of Qingqiu.

**Feng Xi** (封豨) — a colossal boar that had devastated the forests; captured at Sanglin.

**Xiu She** (修蛇/巴蛇) — a vast serpent in the marshes that swallowed elephants whole; severed at Dongting Lake.

Yi swept the world clean. The six monsters fell. The ten suns became one. Yao's civilization was saved.

## What It Cost

Di Jun had sent Yi down to help the lower world. He had given him the bow. He had not told Yi to stop at nine. But the ten suns were also his children — born of his wife Xi He, bathed each night at the Sweet Abyss, raised in heaven. Nine of them were now dead, fallen from the sky like burnt-out stars.

Di Jun could not forgive this. Yi had done exactly what was needed. It was still unforgivable. Di Jun revoked Yi's immortality; he and Chang'e were made mortal and sent to live among humans on earth.

Yi went to the Queen Mother of the West and asked for the drug of immortality. She gave it — enough for two people to ascend to heaven, or enough for one person to become fully immortal. He brought it home. Chang'e took it and fled to the moon.

He was left with the red bow, the empty quiver, the world he had saved, and no path back to the sky.
