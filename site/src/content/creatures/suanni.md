## Pig That Eats Tigers

The Shanhaijing entry is five characters: 狻猊如彘，食虎豹 — "suanni resembles a pig, eats tigers and leopards." No location. No further description. No explanation of how a pig-like creature devours apex predators. This is the Shanhaijing's characteristic mode: the extreme stated flatly, the impossible treated as ordinary.

## The Nine Dragon Sons

The Ming dynasty unified Chinese dragon lore by declaring that the dragon had nine sons, none of which became a dragon themselves, each with a distinct appearance and preference. Yang Shen's 16th-century account is the authoritative list. The suanni is the fifth son. Its preference: 好烟火 — "loves smoke and fire." This is why, Yang Shen explains, its image appears on the legs of incense burners throughout China's Buddhist temples. A creature that loves fire and smoke is the natural guardian of the space where incense burns and prayers rise.

## The Smoke Path

Incense in Chinese religious practice is not merely fragrant — it is the medium through which prayers travel from the human realm to the divine. The smoke is the message. The suanni, positioned at the base of the incense burner or wrapping around its legs, guards this pathway. Its stillness is not passivity but vigilance: it watches every wisp of smoke that rises, ensuring that what is sent above arrives where it was meant to go.

## From Shanhaijing to Japanese Shrine

The suanni's descendant in Japan is the shishi (狛犬, "Korean dog") — the lion-dog guardians found at the entrance to virtually every Shinto shrine in Japan, one with open mouth, one with closed. Millions of shishi figurines exist; they guard homes, businesses, and temples. The line from the Shanhaijing's pig-beast through China's Buddhist temple lions to Japan's ubiquitous shrine guardians is long and winding, but it connects. The creature changed form and function with every border it crossed, keeping only its essential role: something powerful sits near the sacred space and watches.
