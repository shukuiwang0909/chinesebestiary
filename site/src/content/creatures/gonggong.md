## A Son of Fire

The *Classic of Regions Beyond the Seas: North* opens with his court: "Gonggong's minister was named Lord Xiangliu — with nine heads, feeding upon nine mountains." So the Classic's authors knew exactly who Gonggong was, yet in its whole vast circuit they never once show him acting. What they give instead is stranger: a family. The closing genealogy of the *Within the Seas* runs from the Flame Emperor down through Zhurong — the fire god — and Zhurong, descending to dwell by the Jiang river, begot Gonggong. The water god of China is, in the Classic's own arithmetic, the son of fire.

## The Mountain He Broke

His one great scene belongs to the *Huainanzi*. Long ago, the text says, Gonggong fought with Zhuanxu for the sovereignty of the world. Defeated — and here every storyteller savors the sulk of a god who could not bear to lose — he butted Mount Buzhou in his rage. The pillar of heaven snapped; the earth's mooring cords tore; the sky tilted northwest, dragging the sun, moon, and stars after it, while the ground sank in the southeast, and all the waters and silt of the world ran down into the hollow. It is a myth doing triple duty: why heaven tilts, why every great Chinese river runs to the southeast, and why flood follows broken pride. Later tradition stitched the catastrophe into Nüwa's sky-mending — the smash and the repair as a single disaster — but the *Huainanzi* tells each half separately, and the honest reader should too.

## Lord of Floods and His Court

The *Zuo Zhuan* preserves the historical memory under the myth: there was a clan of Gonggong, said to have "regulated by water," and their chiefs figure among the failed contenders of the age of Yao — violent men, one nearly chosen as sovereign, all remembered for stirring floods and ruining the realm. The Classic gives the god his monstrous household: Xiangliu, the nine-headed minister whose appetite flattened nine mountains into swamp, and whose poisoned blood, after Yu the Great cut him down, rendered three whole valleys sterile. Father of the earth god Houtu, master of the nine-headed devourer, scapegoat of the Yellow River's annual tantrums — Gonggong is water at its most ungovernable.

## Fire and Water

Later ages could not leave father and son in peace. The five-phase scheme needed water's champion to stand against fire's, and so Gonggong and Zhurong were recast as natural enemies — a duel modern retellings render with relish, though no early text stages it. The Classic says something more interesting: that water descends from fire, that the flood god fathers the earth god, that destruction and stability are one bloodline. When the pillar broke, the waters ran southeast — and where they settled, silt became farmland, and the world, tilted and drowned and imperfect, became habitable. Every flood plain is, in the old reading, Gonggong's apology.
