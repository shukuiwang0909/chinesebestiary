## The Theft

The Huainanzi, compiled in the second century BCE, records the bare facts: Yi (Hou Yi) asked the Queen Mother of the West for the drug of immortality. His wife Heng'e — later called Chang'e — stole it and fled to the moon. The text says she was "melancholy and bereft" afterward. This is everything the earliest full narrative offers.

The question the text refuses to answer — *why* she took the elixir — has generated two thousand years of interpretation. Two readings have survived in continuous tension.

The first: a man named Feng Meng had studied archery under Hou Yi and then turned against his teacher, intending to steal the elixir by force. Chang'e, unable to overpower him, swallowed the elixir herself to keep it from him. In this reading she is self-sacrificing — she takes immortality not for herself but to prevent catastrophe.

The second: she simply wanted it. Immortality is the greatest gift in the world, and it was there, and she took it when she could. In this reading she is human in the most recognizable way — ambitious, impulsive, willing to bear the consequences of a decision she cannot undo.

The Huainanzi does not choose. It records only what she did and that she was sad afterward.

## The Palace of Vast Cold

The moon she flew to is called 广寒宫 — the Palace of Vast Cold. The name tells you exactly what she found: not warmth, not company, not the garden of the Queen Mother of the West. A cold palace, on a cold moon, in the silence above everything she had known.

She was not entirely alone. The jade rabbit lives on the moon, forever pounding herbs with a mortar, a companion that is also an image of endless pointless labor. Whether the rabbit's work comforts her or mirrors her own condition is a question the poets have been asking ever since.

Qu Yuan's *Questions to Heaven* (天问, c. 4th century BCE) asks about her without expecting an answer:

> 姮娥奔月，何以为此？

"Heng'e fled to the moon — what drove her to this?"

The poem does not answer. No source that preserves her story answers. The question remains open because it is the right question to hold open: what becomes of a person who gains what they wanted and finds they cannot share it with anyone?

## The Mid-Autumn Moon

On the 15th night of the eighth lunar month, when the full moon is at its largest and brightest, the moon is Chang'e's window. Mooncakes are offered. Families gather. The round moon is understood as her face looking down at the harvest she will never gather, on the people she chose to leave, or had to leave, or could not stay with once she had changed.

The festival did not begin as a celebration of her. In its earliest form it was an autumn harvest rite, a general thanksgiving for the completed season. Chang'e entered it gradually, over centuries — the full moon needed a story, the story found a face, and the face was hers. A woman who gave up the earth for the sky, who is closest on the night when the earth celebrates its abundance.

The precise irony of her festival position is this: she is honored on the night that celebrates family reunion, by the woman who is permanently separated from everyone she loved.

## The Space Program

In 2007, China launched Chang'e-1, the first spacecraft in its lunar exploration program, named explicitly for her. The naming was an act of mythological reclamation: the woman who reached the moon first, however she got there, deserved to have her name on the vehicles that followed.

By 2024, six missions had launched. Chang'e-6 was the first in history to return samples from the moon's far side — the hemisphere no one on Earth has ever seen, the side of the moon that faces away from everything.

She was always on that side.
