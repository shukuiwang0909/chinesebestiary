## Four Colors

Most of the Shanhaijing's creatures are described in a single color or two: black body, red head, white tail. The Ba She breaks this pattern:

> 其为蛇青、黄、赤、黑。

"The Ba She is blue, yellow, red, and black."

Four colors, distributed across a body large enough to swallow elephants. Whether this means striped bands, a patchwork of scales in different colors, or a shifting iridescence that includes all four at once, the Shanhaijing does not say. What it records is that no single color could describe the creature — it contains multitudes, literally.

The alternative account says "black snake with a blue head" — a simpler, more biological description, but also more limited. The four-color version has persisted in the imagination because it matches what a creature of this scale seems to require: something too large for a single color to contain.

## The Measurement

The Ba She 食象 — eats elephants. Not an animal the size of an elephant, not a large creature, but specifically the elephant: the largest land animal known to classical Chinese civilization, which inhabited southern China until historical times.

The Shanhaijing gives a specific metabolic detail: 三岁而出其骨 — "after three years it expels the bones." Three years from swallowing to expelling the skeleton. This number does several things at once. It establishes the Ba She as a being on a radically different timescale from human life — it eats perhaps once every three years, its year is a meal. It establishes the completeness of its digestion — after three years, only the bones remain, the rest fully absorbed. And it establishes the status of the bones that remain.

## The Medicine

The most counterintuitive detail in the Ba She's entry is its last: 君子服之，无心腹之疾 — "Gentlemen who consume these bones will have no ailments of the heart or belly."

The bones of an elephant, after three years inside the Ba She, become medicine. They are not poisonous or contaminated — the Ba She's extraordinary digestive system has processed them into something with medical value. The creature that destroys everything it swallows, over three years, produces something that heals people.

This is a characteristic Shanhaijing inversion: the most dangerous creatures are also the sources of the most powerful remedies. The text is full of creatures whose bodies, consumed in the right way, cure diseases or grant protections. The Ba She's case is the most extreme — an elephant, fully processed by a monster over three years, becomes a cure for the body's interior ailments. The scale of the medicine matches the scale of what produced it.

## The Severing

The Huainanzi records that Hou Yi severed the Long Serpent (修蛇) at Dongting Lake as one of his six world-clearing actions. Dongting is the great lake of Hunan province — the second-largest freshwater lake in China, surrounded by the marshlands that southern Chinese tradition associated with water monsters and the extreme margins of the civilized world.

Yi was sent specifically to sever it, not just to kill it. The verb — 断 (duàn), to cut or break — suggests that the serpent was so large that killing it required dismemberment. Perhaps it could regenerate if left whole; perhaps it was so enormous that a single lethal blow was not achievable and it had to be cut apart.

The bones that the severed Ba She left behind, if the Shanhaijing's logic applies, would have been available after Hou Yi's victory. Whether anyone collected them is not recorded.
