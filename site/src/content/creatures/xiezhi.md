## The Court That Could Not Lie To

Every human legal system faces the same problem: humans lie. Witnesses lie, defendants lie, judges can be bribed. The xiezhi solves this with blunt elegance — bring the disputed parties before it and watch which one it gores. No testimony required. No examination of evidence. The animal simply knows.

## From Beast to Badge

The xiezhi entered Han legal symbolism early, but it was the Qing dynasty censorate that made the connection permanent. Censors — the officials charged with investigating corruption at every level of government — wore embroidered xiezhi badges on their official robes. The message was explicit: I hold office as this creature holds justice. My judgment, like its horn, points only at the guilty.

## Korea's Haetae

When the xiezhi crossed to Korea as the haetae (해태), it gained additional powers: protection against fire, floods, and disaster. Stone haetae were placed at the gates of Gyeongbokgung, Seoul's royal palace, where they remain today. In 2009, Seoul city adopted the haetae as its official symbol — making a creature from Han dynasty legal theory into the mascot of a modern metropolis of ten million people.

## The Single Horn

The horn is the creature's entire argument. It is straight (not curved), single (not ambiguous), and points directly forward. In a culture where the bow, the sword, and the axe all have multiple functions, the xiezhi's horn has exactly one: it indicates the direction of guilt. You cannot argue with it. You cannot deflect it. When it turns toward you, you are already convicted.
