## Dijiang, the God of Mount Tian

Deep in the western ranges of the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* — a text assembled between roughly the 4th century BCE and the 1st century CE — Mount Tian rises, rich in gold, jade, and green realgar. There dwells a god the text calls Dijiang, written with the older characters 浑敦, the very word later spelled 混沌. It is shaped like a yellow sack, red as cinnabar fire, walking on six legs and carrying four wings. It has no face — no eyes, no mouth, no openings at all — yet the text insists it knows song and dance. A faceless god who sings: even in that menagerie, there is nothing else like it.

## The Parable of the Seven Orifices

The story that made Hundun world-famous belongs to the *Zhuangzi*, a Warring States text compiled around the 3rd century BCE. Shu (儵, "swift") and Hu (忽, "sudden") — the rulers of the southern and northern seas, both names evoking the blink of an eye — often met in Hundun's realm at the center, and Hundun welcomed them generously. Pitying a host with no face, the two resolved to repay his kindness by boring him the seven openings that humans possess, a new one each day. On the seventh day, Hundun died. The parable is a trap for the well-intentioned reader: what kills chaos is not malice but help — the assumption that every being is merely an unfinished person.

## From Faceless God to Primordial Chaos

By Han times, hundun had outgrown its body. The word became the Chinese name for primordial chaos itself — the shapeless mass in which the world slept before creation. In the Pangu myth as recorded by the scholar Xu Zheng in the 3rd century CE, the first being gestates inside a chaos egg like a chick in its shell, then splits it, the light portion rising as heaven and the heavy sinking as earth. Folk etymology has long whispered that the stuffed pasta hundun (馄饨, wontons) — dough sealed completely around its filling, no opening anywhere — is named for this creature. It is a pleasing association, but a folk one, not settled scholarship.

## The First of the Four Perils

Between beast and concept, Hundun was also enrolled among the villains. The *Zuo Zhuan*, a chronicle commentary completed by the 4th century BCE, names Hundun (written 浑敦) first among the Four Perils (四凶, sì xiōng, "Four Fierce Ones") — monstrous clans the sage-king Shun exiled to the four wildernesses to ward off evil spirits. Its companions in infamy are Qiongqi, Taowu, and Taotie, each with an entry of its own here. Readers who arrive via the *Zhuangzi* may also enjoy Kun-Peng, the giant fish-bird from the same book's opening chapter.
