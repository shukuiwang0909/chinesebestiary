## Not in the Classic — in Its DNA

The honest label first: Bai Ze does not appear in the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*. The omission is almost funny, because the beast is the patron saint of everything a bestiary is. Its home text is the lost *Xuanyuan Benji*, quoted in the Daoist canon: the Yellow Emperor, touring east to the sea and climbing Mount Huan, met on the shore a white beast that could speak and comprehended the nature of all things. So he did what any ruler of the mythic age would do — he interrogated it.

## The Beast Who Knew Everything

The emperor's question was practical: what, exactly, is under my heaven? Bai Ze's answer was a census. From the beginning of things, the essence-breaths that condense into beings and the wandering souls that bring transformations — all of them, every kind — came to 11,520. Bai Ze named them: what each looks like, what each fears, what word or rite masters it. The emperor ordered the knowledge written down and illustrated, and shown to all under heaven. That book was the *Bai Ze Tu* (白泽图), the Bai Ze Illustrations — and it became the fountainhead of every Chinese demonography, ghost-manual, and bestiary written after, the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* included in spirit if not in text. The tradition survives on real paper: Dunhuang copyists of the Tang dynasty produced illustrated *Bai Ze Jingguai Tu* scrolls, and one — manuscript P.2682 — still sits in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, a millennium-old descendant of the emperor's interview.

## The Manual That Warded Off Evil

The catalogue was never just reference. In the old logic, to name a demon correctly is to bind it — and Bai Ze's 11,520 names were therefore 11,520 wards. Tang households hung illustrated Bai Ze scrolls at the gate against night things, and Tang miscellanies record Bai Ze pillows, cushions printed with the beast's likeness, slept on against nightmares: demon-proof headrests, thirteen centuries before merchandise culture. The beast itself receded into the scroll — for what need has a perfect catalogue of a body of its own?

## The Sage's Omen

Like the qilin, Bai Ze appears only to a ruler whose virtue has ordered the world — the beast on the shore is heaven's stamp on the emperor's reign. Later ages kept the discernment and dropped the zoology: the Ming imperial guard carried a Bai Ze banner as the emblem of judgment that sees through deception, and magistrates adopted the beast as patron. Across the sea, Japan received it as the hakutaku — same white body, same encyclopedic smirk, now dosing out diagnoses of demons — while in the Chinese-speaking world, the name survives wherever someone needs a mascot for knowing things cold. This website is, structurally speaking, a Bai Ze Tu; it seems only right to thank the beast on its own page.
