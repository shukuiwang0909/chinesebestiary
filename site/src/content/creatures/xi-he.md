## The Sweet Abyss

At the southeastern edge of the world, where the land ends and the sea begins, there is a place called the Sweet Abyss (甘渊). The water there is sweet — not the salt of the ocean but the pure sweetness of something that has never been spoiled. Every dawn, Xi He brings a sun here and bathes it.

The Shanhaijing records this without explanation:

> 东南海之外，甘水之间，有羲和之国。有女子名曰羲和，方浴日于甘渊。羲和者，帝俊之妻，生十日。

"Beyond the southeastern sea, between the sweet waters, there is the land of Xi He. There is a woman named Xi He, bathing a sun in the Sweet Abyss. Xi He is the wife of Di Jun; she gave birth to the ten suns."

That is the complete description. A woman. A pool. A sun being bathed. The work of the daily dawn, captured in three sentences.

## The Route

The *Huainanzi* supplies what the Shanhaijing omits: the full route of the solar journey. The sun rises from the Valley of Dawn (旸谷), passes through the Salt Pool, brushes against the branches of the Fusang tree — the cosmic mulberry tree where the suns perch between their days on the sky — and then Xi He harnesses six dragons to the celestial chariot and drives it across the dome of heaven from east to west.

The journey passes through six celestial rivers, corresponding roughly to the six-hour intervals of a Chinese day. At the end Xi He arrives at the Ruomu tree, the western counterpart of the Fusang, where the sun rests for the night. Then she returns east to begin again with the next son.

The ten-day Chinese week (旬) comes from this rotation: with ten sons each taking a day in turn, the cycle completes every ten days. Xi He is therefore the origin of one of the oldest time-measurement units in Chinese civilization. Every date on a pre-modern Chinese calendar traces back to her labor.

## After Hou Yi

In the time of the sage-king Yao, all ten suns rose at once. The crops burned. The rivers dried. The world faced catastrophe.

Di Jun gave Hou Yi a red bow and sent him to restore order. Yi shot nine of the suns out of the sky. Nine of Xi He's sons fell.

The Shanhaijing and the Huainanzi record this as fact and then move on. They do not record what Xi He felt. They do not record whether she protested Di Jun's decision to send the archer, or whether she accepted it, or whether she could have stopped it if she had tried. They do not record whether the one remaining sun, driven across the sky every day after that, is comforted or lonely or simply moving because that is what suns do.

What the myth records is the work: Xi He bathed the suns, drove the chariot, maintained the light. The Shanhaijing's silence on her grief is not necessarily the absence of grief — it may be the refusal to claim knowledge the text does not have. Or it may simply be the habit of a text that records what happened and leaves the feeling to the reader.

## Xi He and Chang Xi

The structural symmetry of Di Jun's household is one of the Shanhaijing's most elegant features. Two wives. Two sets of children. Two sets of obligations.

Xi He bathes the suns at the eastern Sweet Abyss and drives them across the sky. Chang Xi bathes the twelve moons at the western sea and tends to the twelve months they represent. East and west, fire and water, day and night — the cosmos is maintained by two mothers working at opposite ends of the world, doing the same work in different light.

Neither is named as the greater or the lesser. Neither is described as being aware of the other. They work in parallel, the sky between them, the daily cycle the product of both their labors.
