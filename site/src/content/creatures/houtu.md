## Three Generations, One Line

The *Classic of the Great Wilderness: Within the Seas* is almost comically terse about the god of the very ground its readers walk on. Gonggong begot Houtu; Houtu begot Yeming; and Yeming begot the twelve years — the whole calendar, running from a flood god through Houtu to the turning months. One line, and yet what a line: it makes the earth the child of water and the parent of time, the still point where the flood settles into season.

## Houtu Becomes the She

The *Zuo Zhuan* supplies what the Classic withholds. In Duke Zhao's twenty-ninth year an official lectures the court on the old religion: there was Goulong, a son of the Gonggong clan, and he served as Houtu — the Earth Rectifier, the fifth and central office of the five-phase priesthood, matching Goumang of wood, Zhurong of fire, Rushou of metal, and Xuanming of water. And then the sentence that built an empire: "Houtu became the She" — the altar of soil. Pair the soil altar with the grain altar and you get sheji (社稷), the twin pillars from which classical Chinese derived its very word for the state. To sacrifice to Houtu was to sacrifice to the ground of the nation.

## The Emperor's Pilgrimage

No rectifier of the five drew monarchs like Houtu. The most storied shrine stood at Fenyin on the banks of the Yellow River, in what is now Wanrong, Shanxi — the Houtu Temple, where Han Wudi came in person in 113 BCE and returned again and again through his reign, offering jade and silk to the god of the soil his whole empire stood on. Tradition credits him there with the "Autumn Wind" song — 秋风起兮白云飞， the great autumn river wind rising — composed on the very banks where earth meets water. The temple was rebuilt dynasty after dynasty; it still stands, one of the oldest surviving state-cult sites in China.

## The Gentle Rectifier

The *Huainanzi* closes its roll of exemplary ministers with him: Goumang, Zhurong, Rushou, Xuanming — wood, fire, metal, water — and finally Houtu, "who never claimed merit, and yet upon him the age of great peace was raised." Water floods, fire burns, wood crowds, metal cuts; earth alone bears everything and says nothing. Later Daoism raised him into her — the Earth Queen, 后土皇地祇, maternal sovereign of all lands beneath the soil, enthroned in every temple that buries the dead. Of all the five rectifiers, only Houtu never needed a dragon: the whole world already rides upon her back.
