## The Bird That Names Itself

The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* hides the bifang in one of its strangest landscapes: Mount Zhang'e (章莪之山), a peak with no grass and no trees, rich in jasper-green jade, and — in the text's own words — a place where "very uncanny things occur" (所為甚怪). The bird shares the mountain with the zheng, a red leopard with five tails and a single horn. Of the bifang itself the text is brisk: crane-shaped, one-legged, red markings on a blue-green body, white beak. Its cry calls out its own name, so that "bifang" rings through the hills before anything burns. Then comes the formula that fixed the creature's fate: *see it, and your district will suffer an ehuo* — a strange fire, glossed by the early commentator Guo Pu as "an uncanny, prodigious flame." The modern mythologist Yuan Ke (袁珂) went further, arguing that the name itself is onomatopoeia — the sharp crackle of bamboo and timber catching fire — and identifying the bifang with the "fire crow" (火老鸦) of later folklore. On this reading the bird is less an animal than combustion given a voice.

## Crane, God, or Essence of Wood?

No single tradition owns the bifang; at least three ancient strands converge on the name, and a bestiary should admit the tangle rather than hide it. The *Classic of Regions Beyond the Seas: South* locates the bird west of the Greenwater and gives it one foot — though some editions arm it with a human face as well (人面一腳). The *Han Feizi*, telling of the Yellow Emperor's great assembly of spirits on Mount Tai, makes Bifang the deity who holds the reins of the imperial elephant carriage — a dignified office, and a much older one than the fire omen. The *Huainanzi* (c. 139 BCE) files it flatly among nature's prodigies: mountains breed the xiaoyang, waters the wangxiang, trees the bifang, wells the fenyang — and people gawk only because they have seen little. Gao You's commentary to that line supplied the reading that dominates later tradition: the bifang is the *essence of wood* (木之精), a blue-green, red-footed, one-legged bird that never touches grain. A third-century gloss on Zhang Heng's "Dongjing fu" disagrees with everyone, describing an "old-father god" shaped like a bird with two legs and one wing, forever carrying fire into people's houses; the Qing scholar Liang Zhangju suspected the text should read one leg and two wings instead. Whether these are one being in decaying memory or several beings fused by a shared name is a question the sources never answer.

## The Exorcist's Fire-Bird

Han and Tang omen culture gave the bifang its working life. The *Wen xuan* preserves Zhang Heng's line on the spirits trembling before "the jiyu and the bifang"; the Ming glossary *Pianya* compresses the whole dossier into four characters — *zhaohuo niao* (兆火鸟), "the bird that foretells fire." The fullest literary portrait is an exorcism: after fires raged night after night in Yongzhou, Liu Zongyuan composed his *Zhu Bifang wen* (c. 806), a formal expulsion addressed to the creature — "a strange bird, none could verify its form... could this be the bifang?" The essay half-believes itself, which is precisely its charm: by the Tang, blaming the bifang was already tradition as much as terror. From such texts and the stage lore built on them, the "one-legged crane" (独脚鹤) hardened into a stock herald of calamity — the bird you draw when you want the audience to smell smoke before the first spark.

## From Omen to Game Sprite

The bifang's modern afterlife keeps the fire. The mobile RPG *Shanhai Jing Hua* (2020) opens its campaign with a half-mad, flame-feathered bifang and keeps it as a playable fire spirit; across games, web fiction, and tattoo flash the name has become shorthand for a beautiful thing that burns what it touches. Popular bestiary culture now sorts it into a taxonomy of Chinese birds: against the fenghuang, the auspicious fire of righteous rule, and Jingwei, the drowning girl's vengeful little bird, the bifang is the calamitous brother — the one whose appearance means no rescue is coming, only the bell and the bucket brigade.
