## The Emperor's Garden

The western mountains of the Shanhaijing are organized as a sequence of increasing divine significance — each range higher and stranger than the last, each guardian more powerful, until the sequence reaches Kunlun, the Lower Capital of Heaven.

Huai Jiang Mountain is one step before Kunlun in this sequence, and on it sits something the Shanhaijing identifies as 帝之平圃 — the Emperor's Flat Garden. "Flat" in the celestial geography of the western mountains is significant: everything around Kunlun is peaks and precipices, and a flat garden suggests a place deliberately cultivated, tended, maintained against the natural geometry of high mountains.

What grows in the Emperor's garden the Shanhaijing does not say. The Kunlun tradition mentions the peaches of immortality, the herbs that can restore life, the trees that mark the boundary between earth and heaven. But Huai Jiang is not quite Kunlun; it is the last garden before the axis of the world.

Its guardian is Ying Zhao.

## The Composite Form

The Shanhaijing describes Ying Zhao with four elements: horse body, human face, tiger stripes, bird wings.

Each element marks a specific domain. The horse body is the power and endurance of the greatest land animal — in Chinese mythology horses are consistently associated with heaven and celestial energy. The human face is the intelligence necessary for a creature entrusted with a specific charge rather than simply roaming free. The tiger stripes carry the authority of the tiger — the most powerful predator in the Chinese world, an animal that does not attack without purpose but is unstoppable once it does. The bird wings are freedom and scope: a creature that needs to patrol the four seas requires the ability to cross them.

The four elements together describe exactly what a celestial garden guardian needs to be. It is not an accidental composite — it is a functional specification.

## Four Seas, One Garden

The Shanhaijing notes that Ying Zhao 徇于四海 — "roams the four seas." The four seas, in Chinese geographical cosmology, are the waters that surround the known world in all four directions. To roam them all is to survey the entire earth.

Ying Zhao's guardianship is therefore not static. It does not stand at the garden gate and wait. It goes out — crosses the world, sees what is there, knows the state of everything — and then returns to its mountain. The patrol circuit is the entire globe.

This gives Ying Zhao a quality that most Shanhaijing guardians lack: worldliness. Lu Wu, just one mountain range east, is fixed at the Kunlun gate. Ying Zhao knows the whole world from the inside. When it returns to the garden, it brings the knowledge of everywhere it has been.

Its call, the Shanhaijing says, sounds like a drum. On its patrol, crossing the sky from one sea to another, that drumbeat sound announces its passage — warning, marking, or simply the sound of something enormous in motion, too large to move silently.
