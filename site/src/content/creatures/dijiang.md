## The God of Mount Tian

The *Classic of the Mountains: West* saves one of its strangest beings for the far end of its westward road. On Mount Tian — beyond which the Ying River flows into the Valley of the Sun, the place where the sun is said to bathe — dwells a god with no face at all. Its body is a yellow sack, its color the red of cinnabar fire, its equipment six feet and four wings. The text's verb for what it lacks is *hundun* (浑敦): muddled, chaotic, unopened. And then, the famous reversal: this faceless, eyeless, earless thing **knows song and dance** (是识歌舞). It is the only being in the entire Classic defined by an art.

## Dijiang and Hundun: One Being or Two?

The question reads backwards through time. In the Classic, *hundun* is simply the adjective describing Dijiang — faceless, unformed. Later readers, meeting the Zhuangzi's parable of the faceless emperor Hundun who died when the gods kindly bored him seven holes, and the *Shiji*'s degenerate emperor "Hunwu," merged the names: Dijiang *is* Hundun, the personification of primal chaos. By the Ming illustrated editions of the Classic the two are drawn and captioned interchangeably — which is why old prints of "Hundun" and "Dijiang" so often show the same faceless fire-sac.

Modern fantasy went the other direction. Post-2006 xianxia webnovels (the *honghuang* "primordial wilderness" school) split Dijiang away from Hundun entirely, promoted him to leader of a council of twelve — the "Twelve Ancestral Witches" (十二祖巫) — and assigned him dominion over space and time. The other eleven members are a grab-bag of genuinely classical gods (Zhulong, Goumang, Zhurong, Gonggong, Houtu, Rushou) and figures from outside the Classic entirely (Xuanming). It is a vivid, influential system — and a modern one, with no basis in any pre-modern text. This entry follows the sources, not the novels.

## Chaos Before the Holes

The Zhuangzi tells the parable that makes Dijiang unforgettable. Hundun, the emperor of the center, treated the emperors of north and south so generously that they resolved to repay him. Noting that every other creature has seven orifices — eyes, ears, nostrils, mouth — and Hundun has none, they bored one hole a day. On the seventh day, Hundun died. The moral lands differently depending on the century: Zhuangzi's is a warning that benevolence applied by a fixed template destroys what it touches; the modern reader more often takes it as the tragedy of a primal being dismantled by categories. Dijiang — sack-bodied, dancing, unbored — is what Hundun looked like before the seventh day.
