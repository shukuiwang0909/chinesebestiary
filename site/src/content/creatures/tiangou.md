## The White-Headed Guardian of Mount Yin

The *Classic of Mountains and Seas*, in its Third Circuit of the Western Mountains, gives the tiangou a single breath: a beast on Mount Yin, shaped like a *li* 狸 — a wildcat or leopard-cat — but with a white head, crying "liu-liu," whose presence "can ward off evil" (可以御凶). It belongs to a recognized class in the classic: the one-eyed, three-tailed *huan* 讙 and the serpent-headed fish *ran yi* 冉遗 are likewise said to keep misfortune at bay. Nothing in the passage is monstrous; the tiangou is closer to a temple guardian than to a demon.

The fourth-century commentator Guo Pu already flags the wobble in the text: some manuscripts give the body "like a leopard" (或作豹) rather than a wildcat, and the cry "mao-mao," a cat's mew (或作猫猫), rather than "liu-liu." The mountain itself is unlocated. Modern commentators have proposed sites in Gansu, Xinjiang, or Qinghai, and one tradition would set Fan Marsh beside a lake in the Qaidam Basin. Note, too, that the same classic opens its Fourth Western Circuit with a *second* Mount Yin, drained by rivers flowing to the Luo — whether one mountain or two bears the name, the text leaves open.

## A Dog-Shaped Star

By the time Sima Qian wrote the *Treatise on the Celestial Offices* (c. 90 BCE), the Heavenly Dog had left the mountains for the sky. There it is a falling star with a tail, a short comet at its side, and a hound's shape beneath — "also an emanation of the metal star Venus" in Meng Kang's Han gloss, the five-phase logic that lets this entry carry the metal element. Where it landed, the ground burned in a circle several hundred *qing* across, and a yellow-tipped blaze foretold armies broken and generals slain a thousand *li* around. The historian even ties the omen to a date: in 154 BCE, as seven states rose against the Han throne, a comet stretched several *zhang* and a tiangou crossed the fields of Liang — and war came.

## The Hound That Swallows the Moon

From that war-star, Chinese folk belief built its most famous dog. By medieval times the "Heavenly Dog" was the creature that swallows the sun and moon: during an eclipse, villagers beat drums, struck gongs, and raised a din to frighten it into spitting the luminaries back out. The custom is genuine and widespread — but a note of honesty is owed: no such eclipse-hound appears in the early classics. It grows from Han omenology and later storytelling; one popular tale makes the dog the faithful hunting hound of the archer Hou Yi, who licked up his master's leftover elixir and flew skyward to harry the moon. The Shan Hai Jing's gentle warding beast and the sky-darkening monster never quite reconcile.

## A Japanese Namesake, and the Confusion It Bred

Across the sea, the story splits. Japan's earliest chronicle, the *Nihon Shoki* (720), already writes 天狗 — pronounced *tengu* — for a meteor, faithfully importing the Chinese astronomical sense. But in Heian Japan the word drifted: tengu became the vengeful spirits (*onryō*) of proud, dead monks, demons of the mountains first drawn with bird wings and beaks. By the medieval period they had absorbed the garb and pride of the mountain ascetics (*yamabushi*), and the Edo era fixed the red face, the forbidding long nose, and the feather fan of the modern tengu — with the crow-like *karasu-tengu* as its feral cousin. This is the lineage behind Sōjōbō, the tengu king of Mount Kurama who taught swordsmanship to the boy Yoshitsune.

Then the borrowed image came home. Through anime, manga, and games — *Nioh*, *Naruto*, and a hundred yokai bestiaries — the long-nosed mountain goblin has flooded back into Chinese popular culture, so that 天狗 today is often assumed to *be* the Japanese creature. The two traditions share a name and nothing more: the Chinese tiangou is a white-headed warding beast and, in a later life, a star of war; the Japanese tengu is a mountain goblin born of medieval ghost-lore. Treat any source that paints the Shan Hai Jing beast with a nose like a ship's prow with suspicion.
