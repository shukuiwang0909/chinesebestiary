## The Leopard-Tailed Lady of Mount Jade

The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* does not describe a gentle queen. It describes a power: human in outline, but with a leopard's tail, tiger's teeth, and a howl she is "skilled at" sending across the mountains (善啸). Her hair is a tangled mane held by a sheng (胜) — a jade hair-ornament. And her office is grim: she "governs the pestilences of Heaven and the Five Ravages" (司天之厉及五残), the star-demons of epidemic and mutilating punishment. The second Classic passage still has her in a cave at Kunlun, at the world's western edge, surrounded by the Weak Water River and a mountain where anything thrown in bursts into flame.

## From Plague-Bringer to Giver of Eternal Life

The turning point comes from outside the Classic. The *Huainanzi* (淮南子, c. 139 BCE) sends the archer Yi to beg the elixir of immortality from her — and tells how Chang'e stole the drug and fled to the moon. In Han dynasty tomb art she is already China's most popular goddess: enthroned, holding a scepter, served by three blue birds, with toad and nine-tailed fox attendant. By the Six Dynasties, the *Han Wu Nei Zhuan* has her descend to Emperor Wu on the Double Seventh carrying peaches that ripen once in three thousand years. The tiger fangs were quietly retired.

## The Feast at Yaochi

Between the two faces sits a love song of empires. The *Mu Tianzi Zhuan* (穆天子传), a Warring States chronicle, sends King Mu of Zhou west with a hundred lengths of silk and a hundred catties of gold; at the Jasper Pool (瑶池) the Queen Mother sings to him: "White clouds are in the sky, the mountains rise of themselves; the road is long, mountains and rivers lie between us — may you not die, and come again" (白云在天，山陵自出). Sixteen centuries later, *Journey to the West* turns her banquet into the cosmic event Sun Wukong crashes. The cave-dwelling calamity goddess of the Classic had become the undisputed head of all female immortals — a promotion unique in Chinese myth.
