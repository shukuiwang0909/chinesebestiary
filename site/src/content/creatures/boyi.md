## The Commission

In the *Book of Documents*, the sage-emperor Shun appoints his ministers one by one. Each receives a specific domain. When it is Boyi's turn, Shun gives him a commission in three characters: 益作朕虞 — "Yi shall be my master of forests and animals." The word 虞 (*yú*) is an office: superintendent of mountains, forests, and wildlife. Boyi is appointed to administer the living world.

The *Records of the Grand Historian* expands on what this meant in practice. Boyi traveled with Shun through the known world, subduing birds and beasts by understanding them — not through force but through a form of communication that the sources consistently treat as his innate gift. Many species were tamed. For this, Shun granted him the surname Ying (嬴) and a status that placed him among the founding ministers of Chinese civilization alongside Yi of the irrigation works, Hou Ji of agriculture, and Yu of the flood.

## Traveling with Yu

When the flood-taming campaign began, Boyi went with Yu the Great. The campaign lasted, by traditional reckoning, thirteen years — years of travel through every mountain range and river basin in the known world. Yu's work was hydrological: channeling water, dredging rivers, establishing drainage. Boyi's work ran alongside: he marked the trees, catalogued the mountains, identified the creatures of each region.

This is the structural basis for the traditional authorship attribution. Liu Xin, writing in the first century BCE, states that Boyi and Yu the Great "first compiled the *Shanhaijing* together." The attribution is almost certainly legendary — modern scholars date the Shanhaijing's composition to the 4th–1st centuries BCE, long after the mythological Boyi. But the logic is sound: a person who spent over a decade traveling with the flood expedition would know exactly what the world contained.

## Author of the Book You Are Reading

The *Shanhaijing*'s traditional authors are Yu the Great and Boyi. Yu handled the geographical and hydrological data; Boyi handled the creatures. When you read an entry in this bestiary — the appearance of a creature, the properties of its meat, the omen it portends — you are, by tradition, reading Boyi's notes.

That tradition makes Boyi the first field naturalist in Chinese history: a person who traveled the world systematically, observed its fauna, and wrote it down. The book attributes no motives to him beyond the observation itself. He saw these things. He recorded them. The encyclopedia is the record of the commission Shun gave him: master the animals by knowing them, and write down what you know.
