## A Race Against the Sun

The whole story runs to fifty characters, and it reads like a myth that knew it would be retold forever. In the *Classic of Regions Beyond the Seas: North*, Kua Fu 逐走 — chases at a full run — and 入日： enters the sun. Whether that means he caught it at the horizon or ran bodily into its burning disc, the text does not pause to say. Thirst, not fire, is what defeats him. He drinks the Yellow River and the Wei down to their beds, turns north toward the Great Marsh, and dies on the road. Then the passage spends its final seven characters on the most famous ending in the entire Classic: 弃其杖，化为邓林 — he cast aside his staff, and it became the Forest of Deng. No moral is appended. A dead giant simply blooms.

## Two Deaths, One Giant

The *Classic of the Great Wilderness: North* tells it again, and disagrees about the ending. It gives Kua Fu a mountain — Chengdu Supports Heaven — a pedigree, and a dress code: he is the grandson of Houtu, 'Sovereign Earth,' and he wears two yellow snakes through his ears while gripping two more in his hands. A neighboring passage sets the land of Kua Fu (a variant reading gives Bo Fu 博父） east of the Long-Eared people, its inhabitants 'great of body,' the Forest of Deng lying to their east. But this second telling also slips in a second, incompatible death: after Yinglong killed Chiyou in the Yellow Emperor's wars, 'he went on to slay Kua Fu,' then withdrew south, 'which is why the south receives so much rain.' The giant who drank rivers dry is finished off by the rain-dragon itself. Later tradition, and modern mythographers such as Yuan Ke, suspect Yinglong belongs to the same archetypal family as Zhulong, the torch-dragon of the northern dark (see the entry on this site) — a convenience of comparison, not a settled fact. The honest position is that the Classic grew in layers; the two Kua Fus are two strata of one archetype.

## Folly or Grandeur

The earliest verdict on the giant is embedded in the Great Wilderness text itself: 不量力， 'not measuring his strength.' The *Liezi* retells the chase under the same shadow, adding only that the staff, steeped in the fat of the corpse, became a forest several thousand li wide. The heroic reading is younger but unstoppable: Tao Yuanming, around 400 CE, opens his ninth 'Reading the Classic of Mountains and Seas' with 'Kua Fu was born with a grand design, and raced the sun on foot,' and closes it by noting that his achievement outlived him — 功竟在身后. The Qing philologist Bi Yuan tried to pin the myth to the map, glossing Deng Forest as the Peach Forest (deng being a sound-loan for peach) by the Hangu Pass; the Classic itself, in the *Classic of the Central Mountains*, sets a Peach Forest of three hundred li square north of a Mount Kua Fu, which lends the gloss real support. In the 1950s, mainland mythography — above all Yuan Ke's *Myths of Ancient China* (1950) — recast Kua Fu as a hero of the laboring people wrestling nature into submission, a reading that marched with the era's 'man's will conquers heaven and earth' rhetoric. Yu Kwang-chung's modernist poem 'Kua Fu' turned the irony around again: why strain to save the dusk at all, when the dusk is only the sun's retreating shadow?

## From Idiom to Orbit

The idiom kuā fù zhú rì (夸父逐日） now sits in dictionaries with both senses at once — a grand ambition, and a fatal overreach — which is as good a summary as any of the argument above. In the 2005 television drama *Jingwei Fills the Sea* he appears as the Star God, guardian of the constellations; across Chinese mythology-themed games he recurs as the ur-sun-chaser, staff in hand. In October 2022 the naming became literal: China's first dedicated solar observatory, the Advanced Space-based Solar Observatory, entered orbit as Kuafu-1 — and when the state had solicited names from the public, a third of the 25,000 proposals said the same thing. The man who died of thirst pursuing the sun is now a machine that watches it without blinking; somewhere between those two images, the staff is still flowering.
