## Two Heads, One Body

The *Classic of Regions Within the Seas* describes Yanwei without preamble: a deity, snake-bodied, human-headed at both ends, dressed for court, as long as a carriage shaft. The political offer follows immediately: host it at a feast and you will become hegemon over the world.

Two heads at opposite ends of one body is a specific philosophical statement. The *Shanhaijing* elsewhere records the undivided (Hundun, the faceless primordial chaos) and the divided (the ten suns, the nine heads of Jiuying, multiple separated entities). Yanwei is neither — it is a creature that is fundamentally two yet inescapably one. You cannot split it, because both ends are the beginning. You cannot say it has a head and a tail, because both ends are the head.

The classical cosmological reading: Yanwei embodies the original unity before heaven and earth divided — the point where yin and yang have not yet separated, where the doubled nature of reality has not yet become two distinct things. A ruler who can receive this creature as a guest has demonstrated the capacity to hold two things together, to unify rather than divide.

## The Purple Robes

A snake in purple court robes and a felt cap is one of the *Shanhaijing*'s stranger juxtapositions. The creature is entirely wild — it lives in marshes, it appears without warning, it is a serpent deity of the deep — but it is dressed for an audience with an emperor. 

Purple was the color of the highest nobility in early Chinese court hierarchy. The felt cap (旃冠 *zhān guān*) was an aristocratic headwear form with nomadic origins, worn at formal occasions. Yanwei is not pretending to be human; it is not wearing these clothes to deceive. It is genuinely courtly — a creature that belongs to the highest order of the divine world, and expresses that belonging through clothing.

## The Zhuangzi's Version

The *Zhuangzi*'s "Mastering Life" chapter tells of Duke Huan of Qi encountering a Weiyi (委蛇) near a marsh. He falls ill from fright. His minister Guan Zhong identifies the creature he saw and explains: seeing it portends great famine for the settlement. Duke Huan recovers upon hearing the explanation — the illness was fear that understanding cured.

In the *Shanhaijing*, Yanwei portends hegemony. In the *Zhuangzi*, Weiyi portends famine. Guo Pu identified them as the same creature. How can the same creature portend two opposite fates?

The answer may be in how one encounters it. Duke Huan stumbled on it in fear. The ruler in the *Shanhaijing* obtains it and hosts it at a feast — a deliberate act of welcome, of treating the divine as a guest. The creature's nature does not change; the encounter changes. The ruler who is ready for hegemony approaches Yanwei differently from the duke who merely stumbles on it.
