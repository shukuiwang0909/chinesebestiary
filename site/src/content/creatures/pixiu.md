## The Beast Without an Exit

The anatomical legend that powers a global amulet industry is simple: the pixiu eats gold, silver, and gems — but has no anus. Once wealth enters, nothing leaves. The Song dynasty feng shui manuals that codified this rule were building on what Han dynasty carvers already felt: something about this winged tiger was fundamentally about accumulation, about a power that takes and does not give back.

## From War to Wealth

In the Han, pixiu were fierce. Yang Xiong compared elite soldiers to 貔貅 — animals of ferocity beyond argument. The Yellow Emperor was said to have trained them as military creatures. Stone pixiu flanked Han tomb gates facing outward, ready to consume any malign spirit that approached the burial. The transition from devourer of demons to devourer of poverty is psychologically clean: the same appetite, redirected inward.

## Two Creatures, One Name

Classical texts distinguish the 貔 (male, Tian Lu 天禄, one antler) from the 貅 (female, Bi Xie 辟邪, two antlers). The male draws wealth from all eight directions; the female repels the five misfortunes. Most modern figurines represent the male alone. The paired form — male left, female right — appears in traditional placement guides and is thought to balance attraction and protection.

## Rules of the Figurine

The modern feng shui ruleset is elaborate: face the pixiu outward toward a window or door; never let it face the bed, mirror, or toilet; activate it by rubbing both eyes while concentrating intention; never let strangers touch a personal pixiu bracelet. The rules' specificity is part of the appeal — a detailed protective ritual is more satisfying than a vague one.
