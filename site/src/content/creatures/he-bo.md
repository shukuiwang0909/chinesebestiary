## The Hymn

The Nine Songs of the Chuci — the anthology of Chu kingdom liturgical poems attributed to the poet Qu Yuan (c. 340–278 BCE) — contain a hymn addressed directly to He Bo. It is one of the most beautiful things in early Chinese poetry:

> 与女游兮九河，冲风起兮水扬波。
> 乘水车兮荷盖，驾两龙兮骖螭。

"I roam with you through the nine rivers, as the rushing wind stirs and the waves rise. I ride in a water carriage with a lotus canopy, harnessed to two dragons with serpents alongside."

The hymn is spoken by a worshipper, describing He Bo's divine river passage. It records his dragon chariot, his lotus-leaf canopy, the wake of fish and turtles behind him, the waves rising at his passage. He is depicted with warmth and grandeur: a god worth addressing directly, worth singing to, worth the careful attention to his beauty.

This is how the southern kingdoms understood their river god — as a magnificent and approachable presence, one who could be reached through song.

## The Parable

The Zhuangzi's *Autumn Floods* chapter uses He Bo for a different purpose.

The Yellow River floods every autumn, swelling until He Bo could not see the far bank. Surveying this, He Bo was pleased. He congratulated himself: his river was the greatest in the world. Then he followed the water east to where it emptied into the sea.

Looking out at the ocean — which has no far bank at all — He Bo fell silent.

The sea spoke to him: "I have heard that there are those who think the Hundred Schools of Philosophy are adequate to explain all knowledge, and themselves sufficient to compare with all others. You just saw your own river — congratulating yourself because of the autumn floods. Is this not a case of the frog in the well praising the width of the sky?"

He Bo said: "Then what is great, and what is small?"

The sea said: "From the perspective of the Dao, things have no noble or base..."

The parable continues for pages. It became one of the most taught passages in Chinese philosophy — a story about the limits of any perspective achieved from within a bounded system. He Bo is the Yellow River, vast by any human measure, until he encounters something unbounded. The lesson is not that the river is small — it is that every perspective is the river.

## The Ritual

The Shiji records what happened in Ye county, in the territory of Wei, for generations before the magistrate Ximen Bao arrived.

The local shamans and elders would gather each year to examine young women. The most beautiful was selected as the "bride of He Bo" — dressed in fine clothes, placed on a reed mat, and floated out onto the Yellow River until she drowned. This was said to please the river god and prevent flooding. It was also, according to Sima Qian's dry account, quite profitable for the shamans who collected fees from the families involved.

When Ximen Bao arrived as magistrate, he attended the ceremony. When the lead shaman came to present the selected bride, Ximen Bao looked at the woman and said she wasn't beautiful enough to please the river god. He asked the shaman to go tell He Bo. He had the shaman thrown into the river. When the shaman did not return, he sent the shaman's assistant to fetch word. The assistant was thrown in too. Then the elders who organized the ceremony were thrown in. Then Ximen Bao waited, as if expecting them back.

Eventually the remaining participants understood that the ceremony was over.

He Bo received no bride that year. The river neither flooded nor did not flood based on any decision He Bo made about it — the Yellow River floods based on rainfall and snowmelt, as it always had. Ximen Bao's act is recorded as the end of the practice.

What is remarkable is that the *Shiji* presents this as a story about a corrupt local priesthood, not as sacrilege against He Bo. The god is never depicted as demanding the sacrifice. The ritual violence was entirely a human invention, done in a god's name without the god's consent, for human profit.
