## The Most Present God

Di Jun appears in the Shanhaijing more often than any other figure — approximately one hundred times across the four chapters of the Great Wilderness. He is listed as the father of the ten suns and twelve moons, the patron of the divine archer Yi, the ancestor of multiple lineages of heroes and craftsmen, the lord over Goumang and Rushou, the great ministers of the eastern and western skies.

He is, without question, the supreme deity of the Shanhaijing.

The *Book of Rites*, *The Records of the Grand Historian*, the *Zhuangzi*, the *Chu Ci*: none of them mention him. He does not appear in the Five Emperors tradition. He has no cult, no temple, no ritual. He is the most powerful figure in China's oldest mythological encyclopedia and the least acknowledged figure in every tradition that followed it.

This is the puzzle at the center of Di Jun's myth.

## Two Wives and a Sky Full of Children

The Shanhaijing preserves a cosmological system built on Di Jun's family:

**Xi He** (羲和), his first wife, gave birth to the ten suns. Every morning she drove one of her sons across the sky in a dragon-drawn chariot, bathing him at dawn in the Sweet Abyss of the east. The ten suns were meant to take turns — one per day, in rotation. When all ten appeared at once in the time of Yao, the world nearly burned.

**Chang Xi** (常羲), his second wife, gave birth to the twelve moons. The *Shanhaijing* records her bathing them in the west, one by one, their light renewed. The twelve moons became the twelve months of the lunar calendar.

Di Jun is therefore the father of all the light by which China marked its time. Every year, every month, every day was made of his children. When Hou Yi shot nine of the ten suns out of the sky, he killed nine of Di Jun's sons.

## The Red Bow

Di Jun gave Hou Yi a red bow (彤弓) and white-fletched arrows and sent him down to the lower world to relieve its hardships. This is recorded in the Great Southern Wilderness chapter of the Shanhaijing as though it were a matter of routine: the supreme deity issued a commission and sent his agent.

What the Shanhaijing does not record — and what the Huainanzi supplies — is what happened to Di Jun when Yi fulfilled that commission. Yi shot nine of the ten suns. Nine of Di Jun's sons fell from the sky. The earth was saved. Di Jun stripped Yi of his divinity.

The calculation is laid bare: the world's survival required the death of most of its sunlight. Di Jun chose to save the world. He then made someone pay for it. This is not the behavior of a forgiving god, but it is the behavior of a parent.

## The Disappearance

After the Zhou dynasty, Di Jun effectively ceases to exist in Chinese religious and literary tradition. The Yellow Emperor (Huang Di) becomes the supreme patriarch of the Confucian Five Sovereigns. The jade emperor of the Daoist tradition is unconnected to Di Jun's lineage. The Buddhist heavens have their own hierarchy.

Scholars have debated for a century whether Di Jun was absorbed into another figure — perhaps Di Ku, one of the Five Emperors, who also has two wives who give birth to solar and lunar beings — or deliberately displaced as the Yellow Emperor tradition consolidated political and cosmological authority. The Shanhaijing was compiled at the margins of the emerging Han synthesis; Di Jun may be a preserving fossil of an older cosmological order that the new state chose not to continue.

The Shanhaijing keeps him exactly as he was: the god who ran heaven, who fathered the light, who sent the archer to save the world, who paid what it cost — and then simply stopped being remembered.
