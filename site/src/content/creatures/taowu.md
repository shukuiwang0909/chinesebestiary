## The Unteachable Son

The *Zuo Zhuan* — a chronicle of the Spring and Autumn period, compiled around the 4th century BCE — delivers the earliest verdict on taowu. He began as a man: the unworthy son of Zhuanxu, one of the ancient Five August emperors. No teacher could reach him; no words would register. Correct him and he turned mulishly defiant (顽); leave him alone and he grew brazen and loud (嚚). He scorned bright virtue itself and threw Heaven's constant order into confusion, so the people named him Taowu. In the same passage, the sage-king Shun drives him out with three fellow reprobates to the four edges of the world to fend off demons — the act that assembles the Four Perils.

## The Beast of the Western Waste

Centuries later, the *Shen Yi Jing* — a Han-era bestiary attributed to the court wit Dongfang Shuo — gives the incorrigible son a body. In the wilderness of the west lives a beast shaped like a tiger but bristling with dog hair, only two chi long (less than half a metre), with a human face, tiger paws, a pig's snout armed with tusks, and a tail one zhang and eight chi long — over four metres, nine times the length of its body. It throws the whole wasteland into uproar. The small body and absurd whip of a tail have made it a favourite of later illustrators.

## Obstinacy, Not Gluttony

Chinese readers sorted their four great monsters the way naturalists sort species. Taotie is appetite — the maw that devours everything it sees. Qiongqi is perversity — it punishes the good and rewards the wicked. Hundun is chaos itself, faceless and formless. Taowu's sin is different and, to a Confucian state, worse: he cannot be taught. Correction slides off him like rain off a stone. Two millennia on, his name still means pig-headed, irredeemable wrongness — a dictionary byword for the stubbornly wicked, and in parts of southern China still hurled at a fool who will not learn.

## From Monster to Book Title

The word outgrew the beast. The *Mencius* (c. 3rd century BCE) notes that while Lu kept its *Spring and Autumn* and Jin its *Sheng*, the state of Chu called its official chronicle the *Taowu* — the monster's name repurposed for the raw logs of history, from which lessons were hewn. In the late Ming, an anonymous court novel about the corrupt eunuch Wei Zhongxian took the title *Idle Criticism of Taowu* (梼杌闲评): history, monstrosity, and blame folded into a single word — exactly how the word has always worked.
