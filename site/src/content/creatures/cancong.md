## The King Who Looked Differently

Of the five legendary dynasties of ancient Shu, only the first is remembered by a physical description. The *Chronicle of the Kings of Shu* preserves it in six characters: 其目纵，始称王 — "his eyes vertical, he was the first to be called king." Nothing else is said about Cancong's appearance, deeds, or reign. The character of a civilization compressed into two features: the eyes, and the throne.

## What "Vertical Eyes" Means

The phrase 纵目 (zòng mù) has generated more archaeological debate than almost any other line in Chinese legendary history. *Zòng* means vertical, longitudinal, or projecting forward — the same character used for a lance thrust and for the movement of rivers. Eyes that are *zòng* are not merely large or bright: they are eyes that extend into space in a direction that ordinary eyes do not.

The Sanxingdui Bronze Age culture — excavated beginning in 1986 from a site in Guanghan, Sichuan, roughly 40 kilometers from Chengdu — produced exactly one category of object that matches this description with no ambiguity: the 纵目铜面具 (protruding-eye bronze mask). The most dramatic example extends its cylindrical bronze pupils 16 centimeters forward from the face, on stalks that have no parallel anywhere in the Bronze Age world. No Shang vessel mask, no Zhou bronze, no Mesopotamian or Egyptian artifact uses this convention. The Sanxingdui bronzes were made by people who had a specific theological or iconographic reason to depict eyes this way.

## What Archaeology Cannot Confirm

The connection between Cancong and the Sanxingdui masks is powerful and widely cited, but it is inference, not text. The Sanxingdui site left no writing. The masks were not found with labels. The Chronicle of the Kings of Shu was written a thousand years after the Sanxingdui culture declined, by an author working from oral tradition in the Han dynasty.

The honest reading: Cancong's 纵目 description is the oldest textual evidence for a "vertical-eyed" tradition in Shu; the Sanxingdui masks are the oldest *visual* evidence for the same tradition. Whether one came from the other, whether both came from a third tradition, or whether the Han-era chronicler heard old stories and the Sanxingdui masks illustrate those same stories independently — remains open.

## The Forgotten Kingdom

Li Bai's poem *Difficult Road to Shu* (蜀道难) begins: *Cancong and Yufu founded this country — who can say when? Eight and forty thousand years have passed, and no traveler crossed to the Qin borderland.* The forty-eight thousand years is literary exaggeration; the isolation is not. The Shu kingdom developed independently of the Central Plains' Shang and Zhou cultures, sharing little mythology and no writing system. Its cosmology — if the Sanxingdui bronzes are read as cosmological — featured giant sacred trees, standing human-figure statues almost two meters tall, gold foil masks, and jade discs of a kind not found in Shang ritual contexts.

When the Qin conquered Shu in 316 BCE, most of this tradition stopped accumulating. The texts that survived were late, fragmentary, and already puzzled by what they were describing. Cancong arrived in the record as a name, two features, and a kingdom that the literary tradition measured in geological time because no human scale seemed adequate.
