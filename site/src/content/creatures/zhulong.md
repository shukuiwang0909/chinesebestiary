## The Highest God of the North

The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* describes the supreme deity of the northern world twice, in two different books. The *Classic of Regions Beyond the Seas: North* calls him Zhuyin, the god of Mount Zhong; the *Classic of the Great Wilderness: North* calls him Zhulong, the Torch Dragon, of Mount Zhangwei. Everything about the two descriptions matches — the human face, the red serpent body, the refusal of food and sleep, the lighting of darkness — and early readers already fused them. The 3rd-century commentator Guo Pu glossed the first passage with a single note: "the Torch Dragon — he torches the Nine Darknesses, and is named from this." The Qing philologist Hao Yixing, in his *Shanhaijing jianshu* (山海经笺疏), treated Mount Zhong and Mount Zhangwei as one mountain and the two names as one god. What we cannot recover is whether the two passages descend from a single older source or were harmonized by exactly such commentators.

## A Face on a Thousand-Li Serpent

He is a thousand *li* long — roughly five hundred kilometers — a creature that is also a landscape. His face is human; his body is the red of embers. The *Great Wilderness* passage adds the enigmatic phrase "straight eyes, upright-cheng" (直目正乘): Guo Pu explained the straight eyes as vertical ones (目纵) and confessed, with rare honesty, that he had never heard what *zheng cheng* meant. Later phonologists have proposed reading *cheng* (乘) as a loan for *zhen* (朕), "a straight seam" — eyes that shut into a single narrow slit — but the phrase remains unsettled, and this bestiary will not pretend otherwise. His body, too, shifts between sources: the Shanhaijing itself gives a snake's body (蛇身), while the *Huainanzi* gives a dragon's body with no feet (龙身而无足); Guo Pu, quoting a lost edition, even transmitted the reading "dragon body, one foot," which Hao Yixing dismissed as a scribal slip.

## The Dragon Before Pangu

No other creature in the classic commands the functions of the cosmos itself. Day and night turn on his eyelids; winter and summer ride on his breath; wind is the exhaust of his existence. This is creation-grade mythology, and the great 20th-century collator Yuan Ke argued that the much later Pangu myth — first written down by Xu Zheng's *Sanwu lianji* (三五历记) in the 3rd century CE — grew out of precisely this older stratum: when the giant Pangu dies, his eyes become sun and moon and his breath the wind and weather, a physiology the Torch Dragon had already embodied a thousand years earlier in writing. A rival, naturalistic current in modern scholarship reads the thousand-li red serpent flickering over the polar night as a memory of the aurora borealis; it is an attractive guess, and only a guess. Around the god clusters an underworld imagery: the *Chu ci* sends the soul away from the north, where "the Coiling Dragon glows red" on the Cold Mountain (逴龙, usually read as Zhulong); Wang Yi's Eastern Han commentary describes "a dark, sunless land in the northwest of the sky, with a dragon holding a torch to light it"; the Han apocryphon *Shi han shenwu* (诗含神雾) sends "a dragon carrying a fire-essence in its mouth to light the Gate of Heaven"; and Gao You's commentary on the *Huainanzi* completes the fusion — "the dragon holds a torch to light the moon-darkness, some thousand li long." Tellingly, the *Great Wilderness: North* plants Mount Zhangwei in the same landscape as the Nine Yin Mountain (九阴山) and the red Ruo tree (若木) of sunset — the dragon who torches the Nine Darknesses dwells beside the geography of dusk.

## Modern Torchlight

The Torch Dragon has become the default Chinese "time dragon." Xuan-Yuan Sword V (2006) put Zhuyin on screen straight from the bestiary; the open-world game Chimeraland (2021) made him a creation god on Mount Zhangwei, the myth's own mountain; and Shanghai's Aurogon studio — 上海烛龙, literally "Shanghai Torch Dragon" — took his name before building the Gujian series. In Chinese web fiction the formula "his eyes open — it is day; his eyes shut — it is night" (睁眼为昼，闭眼为夜) has become a stock phrase for world-shaking beings, and anime-style games cast him as an ancient dragon of time. Few readers of those scenes may know how much of the trope is verbatim three centuries before the common era.
