## The Valley of the Rising Sun

The eastern edge of the Shanhaijing's world is organized around the sun's beginning. The Valley of the Rising Sun (朝阳之谷) is where the day starts — not merely a direction on a map but a cosmological location, the point where the sun enters the sky each morning after Xi He's bathing and preparation.

In this valley, or between the two rivers north of the Xie marker near it, the Shanhaijing places Tian Wu:

> 朝阳之谷，神曰天吴，是为水伯。

"In the Valley of the Rising Sun, the spirit is called Tian Wu — this is the Water Earl."

"Water Earl" (水伯) is a title, not just a description: Tian Wu is the feudal lord of the eastern waters, the governing authority over the rivers and seas of the part of the world where the day begins. The sun rises over Tian Wu's domain every morning. The eastern sea is its jurisdiction.

## The Eight

Eight heads. Eight legs. Eight tails.

The number eight carries specific significance in Chinese cosmology — the eight trigrams of the Bagua map the eight fundamental forces of existence; the eight directions (四方四隅, four cardinal and four intercardinal) divide space completely; the eight is the complete spatial and cosmological set.

Tian Wu's eight-fold multiplicity is not a description of a freak but a description of a cosmic function. A deity that governs the eastern waters in every direction — north, south, east, west, northeast, northwest, southeast, southwest — needs eyes in all eight. Eight human faces arranged in a ring, all watching, all aware: nothing in the eastern sea escapes its notice.

The Great Eastern Wilderness version gives a tiger body and ten tails — a slight difference that likely reflects regional variants in how the deity was conceived. The important elements — the multiplicity, the human faces, the aquatic domain — remain constant across both accounts.

## Before the Dragon Kings

Tian Wu is the Water Earl of the East at a time before the Dragon King system organized all of China's water governance into four neat courts.

In the later tradition — crystallized in Tang and Song dynasty literature and reaching its most complete form in *Journey to the West* — four Dragon Kings rule the four seas: Ao Guang in the East, Ao Qin in the South, Ao Run in the West, and Ao Shun in the North. Each court has officials, records, and bureaucratic hierarchy. The water is administered.

Tian Wu predates this. It is a creature-deity rather than an official — it governs through presence and power, not through a court with seals and paperwork. The Shanhaijing's water governance is pre-bureaucratic: Tian Wu in the east, He Bo in the Yellow River, Gonggong in the great floods. Each is a being rather than an institution.

When the Dragon King system arrived, it did not exactly replace these older water deities — it organized around them, above them, or alongside them. Tian Wu remained in the Shanhaijing's geography, governing the Valley of the Rising Sun at the world's edge, outside the administrative district of any Dragon Court.

## Two Traditions

The discrepancy between the *Seas Beyond the East* account (eight heads, beast body, blue-green and yellow) and the *Great Eastern Wilderness* account (tiger body, ten tails) is characteristic of the Shanhaijing's method of preservation.

The Shanhaijing is a composite text assembled from multiple regional sources over several centuries. Where two traditions about the same deity had been preserved, the text often includes both without attempting to reconcile them. Tian Wu appears in both versions because both versions had authority in their respective regional traditions.

The compiler's decision to preserve the contradiction rather than smooth it tells us something: the goal was completeness, not consistency. Both accounts of Tian Wu are true in the sense that both were believed by people whose accounts were worth recording.
