## A Princess Drowned in the Eastern Sea

Inside the dry inventory of the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* — mountain after mountain, river flowing into river — the entry for Mount Fajiu stops the reader cold. A bird shaped like a crow, white-beaked and red-footed, cries out its own name. The text then explains, in one compressed sentence, what that cry means: this is Nüwa, the youngest daughter of the Flame Emperor (Yandi), who went swimming in the Eastern Sea, drowned, and never came back. What returned instead was Jingwei.

Note the honesty of the source. The Shanhaijing names no villain: no dragon, no negligent sea god — only water, and a girl it killed. Her vengeance is aimed at the sea itself, the nearest thing to an indifferent cosmos that archaic China could picture.

## The Impossible Task

Her project is mathematically absurd: to dam the Eastern Sea with twigs and pebbles carried from the Western Hills, hundreds of li away. The text states it flatly — ever carrying wood and stone, to dam up the Eastern Sea — with no promise of success and no stated ending. That flatness is the power of the myth. Later readers supplied the moral. Guo Pu's 4th-century encomium makes it explicit: her body sank, her bright spirit journeyed west — and then she went back to work. The dead do not rest; they file appeals.

## Vows, Mates, and Other Names

By the time of Ren Fang's 6th-century *Records of Marvels* (述異記), folklore had thickened around the bird. Jingwei calls her own name as she flies; she swore never to drink from the water that drowned her, and the spot of that vow was still being pointed out on the coast. Finding no mate of her own kind, she paired with a storm petrel — daughters born shaped like Jingwei, sons like the petrel. The tradition piles on epithets that read like a personality study: the Vow Bird (誓鳥), the Wronged Bird (冤禽), the Bird of Resolve (志鳥), and the homely folk name Emperor's Daughter Sparrow (帝女雀). One transmitted edition even garbles the first of these into 鳥誓 — a small reminder that our textual base for 'minor' myth is thin and slippery, and this bestiary prefers to say so.

## From Wronged Bird to National Metaphor

The decisive re-reading came from Tao Yuanming (c. 400 CE), whose poem pairs Jingwei with Xingtian, the headless warrior still brandishing shield and axe: 'fierce resolve endures forever.' Where the Shanhaijing offers an unsettling vignette, Tao offers a doctrine — persistence as a form of immortality. Yu Xin's 6th-century *Lament for the South* turns it tragic: can the wronged bird ever fill the sea? And Lu Xun, in his 1935 essay drafts, cited Tao's very lines to argue that China's great quietist was 'not all serenity.'

By the twentieth century, 精卫填海 — 'Jingwei fills the sea' — had crystallized into a standard chengyu for dogged, seemingly hopeless perseverance, and the story was taught to every schoolchild as the parable of the unbreakable will. One caution for the careful reader: it is easy, and probably wrong, to file the myth as simple inspiration. The oldest text leaves its queasiness intact. A child is dead, the sea does not notice, and the work goes on anyway. Whether that is heroism or open trauma depends on which shore you stand on.
