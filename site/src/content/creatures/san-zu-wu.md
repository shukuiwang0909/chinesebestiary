## One Bird Per Sun

The *Classic of the Great Eastern Wilderness* locates the beginning of each day at a specific place: the Hot Spring Valley, east of the Black-Teeth people, where the cosmic Fusang mulberry tree grows with its roots in the sea and its crown touching the sky. Each morning, Xi He — wife of the celestial emperor Di Jun, mother of the ten suns — draws one of her sons up in the sun-chariot and drives him across the sky. The other nine wait in the lower branches of the Fusang tree: nine bright discs, roosting.

Inside each disc rides a crow.

The *Huainanzi* says it plainly: 日中有踆乌 — "Inside the sun there is a crouching crow." Later texts give the bird a name: 金乌 (jīn wū), Golden Crow, or 三足乌 (sān zú wū), Three-Legged Crow. The third leg is the diagnostic detail — no ordinary crow has three legs, and the anomaly marks divine origin. When Qu Yuan's *Questions to Heaven* (c. 4th century BCE) asks "Where did Yi shoot through the suns? Where did the crow shed its feathers?" it assumes the crow is already known. The question is only where the feathers fell.

## The Shooting

The crisis began when all ten suns appeared in the sky at once. The earth burned. The crops failed. The six world-threatening monsters ranged freely. Di Jun, their father, commissioned the archer Yi — gave him a red bow and white arrows — and sent him down to relieve the suffering.

Nine suns fell. Nine crows fell with them.

The *Questions to Heaven* compresses this to two lines. Each shot struck a sun and a crow at once — the solar disc and the divine bird inside it. When the crow fell, its feathers scattered. Nine sets of solar feathers scattered across the burning world as Yi worked through the sky.

The tenth sun survived because Yao, the sage emperor, grabbed Yi's arm. One sun was enough. One crow remained, riding its chariot across the sky each day, and it rides there still.

## Symbol of the Sun

In the Han dynasty (202 BCE–220 CE), the three-legged crow became one of the standard solar emblems of Chinese court art. It appears stamped into bronze mirrors, carved into jade pendants, and painted in the ceiling tiles of tombs — wherever the sun was to be depicted, the three-legged crow appeared inside the solar disc. The creature was not worshipped in the way the dragon or the fenghuang was; it was used, the way a symbol is used. It meant *sun*, the way a disc with rays means *sun* in Western iconography.

The difference is that the Chinese sun had legs. Three of them.

## East Asia's Solar Crow

No creature in the *Shanhaijing* traveled further from its source. The three-legged solar crow moved east along the same continental routes that carried Chinese script, bronze technology, and Buddhism.

In Japan, the tradition became the **Yatagarasu** (八咫烏) — a three-legged divine crow sent by Amaterasu, the sun goddess, to guide the first emperor Jimmu during his eastern campaigns. The *Nihon Shoki* (720 CE) records the guidance but not the three legs explicitly; later iconography fills them in. The Yatagarasu eventually became the emblem of the Japan Football Association, appearing on the national team's crest — the same solar crow, still pointing the way, now carried by athletes before a global audience.

In Korea, the Goguryeo kingdom (37 BCE–668 CE) painted the three-legged crow in its royal tomb murals as a solar symbol, where it appears alongside the phoenix and the dragon in the celestial hierarchy. The Goguryeo crow — Samjokgo (삼족오) — is now the national symbol of the Korean martial arts organization and a recurring motif in Korean nationalist art.

Three countries, three names, one bird. The Chinese tradition is the oldest, but the solar crow does not belong to one culture. It belongs to the sky.

## What the Feathers Meant

The nine fallen crows represent an irreversible subtraction from the cosmos. Before Yi's campaign, the world had ten solar birds. After it, one. The arithmetic of the myth is not triumphant — it is a record of what was permanently removed from the world in order to save it.

Yi did not know this when he drew his bow. He shot the suns because he was asked to, because the commission came with the authority of a red bow from the celestial emperor, and because the world was burning. Nine birds fell. Nine suns went dark. The feathers are still scattered somewhere, according to Qu Yuan's unresolved question — still lying where they landed in whatever mythological geography corresponds to the moments after the sky was fixed.
