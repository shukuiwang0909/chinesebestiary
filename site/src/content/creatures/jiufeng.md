## Nine Heads at the Top of the World

The *Classic of the Great Wilderness: North* climbs to the very edge of its map — a mountain called the Celestial Chest of the Utmost North (北极天柜), where the sea waters pour away into the north — and perches one being upon it: a spirit with nine heads, a human face, and the body of a bird. Its name is Jiufeng, the Nine Phoenix. That is all the Classic says. No exploit, no appetite, no omen — just a creature with nine human faces riding one vast avian body, keeping watch where the world drains off its edge.

## From Phoenix to Ghost Cart

Later centuries lost their nerve about it. By the Six Dynasties, the same nine-headed night bird was haunting folklore under new names — the Ghost Cart (鬼车), the Nine-Headed Bird (九头鸟) — and the transformation is a small tragedy of medieval Chinese religion. The old northern wilderness, once crowded with gods, was reclassified as demon country as the civilized map crept south; beings like Jiufeng were demoted with it. The folklore Jiufeng screams like a cart-axle at night, flaps on pinions that drip blood, and passes over a roof only when the household beneath is marked for a death within the year. The seasonal countermeasures survive in the *Seasonal Rites of Jingchu* (6th century): douse every lamp, beat the beds and doors, and twist the household dogs' ears so their howls drive it off. One head, the tale adds, a heavenly dog had already bitten away.

## Nine as a Number

Stack a sacred number on a living body and wonder tips into dread — the same arithmetic that makes the nine-tailed fox uncanny works here with interest. Nine is the supreme yang number, the emperor's number, and the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* gives nine-headed company elsewhere: Xiangliu the nine-headed serpent, devouring mountains in the north, is Jiufeng's dark mirror — same count of heads, same quarter of the map, opposite verdicts of beauty and horror. The Classic merely notes what stands at the top of the world; it is its readers who decided whether to kneel or to bar the door.

## The Bird That Refused to Die

The Nine-Headed Bird never left the language. Central-Chinese proverb still insults a schemer as a jiutouniao — 'he has as many heads as the nine-headed bird,' too many angles at once — and in Hubei and Hunan the saying 'Heaven saw the Nine-Headed Bird, earth saw the Hubei merchant' turned a demon into a backhanded emblem of local cunning. Modern guofeng games and illustration have begun restoring the older, grander reading: not a blood-dripping curse, but an arctic phoenix with nine serene faces, watching from the Celestial Chest as the northern sea pours away.
