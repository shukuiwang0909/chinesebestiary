## Dragon Head, Human Body

The *Shanhaijing*'s mountain deities come in two standard forms: some have human faces and animal bodies; others have animal heads and human bodies. The distinction maps onto different kinds of divine authority. A human face on an animal body suggests a deity whose wisdom mediates the wild — it can see you as you see it. An animal head on a human body suggests something different: a deity whose essence is its elemental nature, wearing human form as a garment but carrying its true character in the head.

Jimeng is the second type: human body, dragon head. The dragon head is the Shanhaijing's consistent marker for water and weather control. It appears on river gods, sea deities, and storm spirits. It is the anatomy of rainfall. Jimeng carries it above a human body, standing upright in the Zhang abyss at the bottom of the Min Mountains — a deity with a person's posture and a dragon's face, waiting at the bottom of a deep pool.

## The Storm as Emergence

The text's description of Jimeng's storm effect is precise in a way that matters: "whenever it enters or exits, there are always violent winds and torrential rain." Not "it summons storms" or "it controls weather." The storm is a consequence of *movement*. When Jimeng descends into the Zhang abyss, a storm follows. When it rises from the abyss, a storm follows. The deity's transition between worlds is itself the meteorological event.

This is observationally coherent. Mountain storms in the Min range — which runs along the eastern margin of the Tibetan Plateau and forms the western boundary of the Sichuan Basin — are frequently sudden and localized. A valley can generate a thunderstorm in minutes while the ridge above it stays clear. The experience of a storm that seems to come from inside a gorge, from the deep pool at the valley's bottom, is what Jimeng's story explains.

## Mount Jimeng

The mountain named for this deity is one of sixteen in the Min range catalogued in the ninth central chapter. Its top is rich in gold and jade; its lower slopes produce two notable species:

**Jiang tree** (橿): crabapple form, mulberry leaves, yellow flowers, red fruit. Eating the fruit prevents you from running but makes your horses faster — a pharmacological tradeoff that the Shanhaijing records without judgment.

**Rongcao** (荣草, "glory grass"): orchid leaves, bellflower root, black flowers, no seeds. Taking it removes sorrow.

The grass that removes sorrow grows on a mountain where storms erupt whenever the god moves. The Shanhaijing does not comment on this conjunction.
