## The Thunder Marsh

The Shanhaijing locates the Thunder God with cartographic specificity: in the Thunder Marsh (雷泽), west of the Wu region. Thunder Marsh is a real place — a wetland in what is now eastern China — and this gives the Shanhaijing's Thunder God a quality that the later Daoist Lei Gong lacks: a specific home.

The text describes the creature:

> 雷泽中有雷神，龙身而人头，鼓其腹，在吴西。

"In the Thunder Marsh there is the Thunder God: a dragon body with a human head, who drums on its belly. It is west of Wu."

This is the original form: not the bird-beaked official with wings and a drum mallet, but a dragon-bodied being who generates thunder by striking its own abdomen. The belly-drumming is ancient — it recalls the Yellow Emperor's use of the Kui's hide as a drum, and the notion that cosmic sounds come from the bodies of divine creatures rather than manufactured instruments.

## From Creature to Official

The transition from the Shanhaijing's dragon-bellied Thunder God to the Daoist tradition's Lei Gong — blue-skinned, bird-beaked, winged, mallet-wielding — took roughly a thousand years.

Qu Yuan's *Li Sao* (c. 4th century BCE) already names the "Thunder Master" (雷师) as a bureaucratic official in the divine court, suggesting that the personification and institutionalization was underway in the Warring States period. By the Tang and Song dynasties, Lei Gong had fully entered the Daoist celestial bureaucracy, working alongside Dian Mu (Lightning Mother), Feng Bo (Wind Lord), and Yu Shi (Rain Master) as coordinated divine storm officials.

The earlier, wilder creature form did not disappear — it survived in the consciousness that thunder has something fundamentally animal about it, something that precedes human administration.

## The Moral Enforcement Function

The most distinctive development in Lei Gong's later mythology is moral: he became the divine executioner who strikes down the wicked whose crimes are hidden from human justice.

The logic is simple and effective. Thunder comes suddenly. Its target cannot be predicted. It strikes outdoors, unpredictably, as if guided by something that can see through walls and social propriety to what a person has actually done. Popular Chinese religion interpreted sudden thunder strikes as divine execution — Lei Gong had identified a crime that human courts had missed.

Specifically, he was associated with the punishment of those who poisoned others — a crime notoriously difficult to prove in pre-modern justice systems. Thunder hitting a house was sometimes interpreted as confirmation that someone inside had been poisoning their neighbors. This made Lei Gong the deity of hidden justice: not the justice people could see and verify, but the absolute accounting that heaven keeps separately from human record.

## The Battle of Zhuolu

The Shanhaijing connects Lei Gong's storm team to the most significant battle in Chinese mythology. When Chi You fought the Yellow Emperor, he summoned Feng Bo and Yu Shi — Wind Lord and Rain Master — to generate catastrophic storms. The storm team answer to the same divine administration as Lei Gong; whether Lei Gong himself participated is not explicitly stated, but the storm system deployed against the Yellow Emperor is the same one Lei Gong coordinates in later tradition.

The Yellow Emperor's counter was Nu Ba, the drought goddess, who dried the storm and allowed the battle to be won. The implication is that Lei Gong's storm team, if fully deployed, was beyond the Yellow Emperor's military capacity to counter — he needed a specific drought deity, not a general weapon.
