## One Line, One God

The *Classic of Regions Beyond the Seas: East* spends exactly seven characters on Goumang (东方句芒，鸟身人面，乘两龙): he presides over the east, he has a bird's body and a human face, and he rides two dragons. That is the whole entry — yet it was enough. The line is part of a set: east Goumang on two dragons, south Zhurong on two dragons, west Rushou with a snake in his left ear on two dragons, north Yuqiang with snakes at ears and feet. The four directional gods share one chassis and differ in their trim, as if the Classic's compilers were cataloguing the officers of a single celestial bureaucracy.

## Wood Rectifier

The office came from the Zuo Zhuan. In Duke Zhao's twenty-ninth year an official explains the old religion to a skeptical court: each of the five phases has its rectifier (正), and the Wood Rectifier is called Goumang. The same passage preserves his one surviving miracle-story. Duke Ping of Jin fell ill and dreamed of a yellow bear entering his bedchamber; later, ill again, he dreamed of a bird-bodied, human-faced god riding two dragons — Goumang himself. The dream-interpreter congratulated him: the god of life-span had appeared, and the duke's allotted years would run their full course. When the duke demanded a human sacrifice to seal the bargain, the minister Ji Zha rebuked him — the harvest of the five phases belongs to the people, and it is good government, not slaughter, that persuades heaven. Duke Ping lived, and the anecdote became the standard proof that Goumang governs how long a life may run.

## Spring, Birds, and the East

The *Monthly Ordinances* (月令) of the Book of Rites turned Goumang into ritual procedure: in the first month of spring the ruler must greet the east in person, reward the officials of agriculture, and forbid all executions — because the east is the sector of wood, and wood is the phase of everything starting to grow. The bird body fits the same logic: the east is where the sun and its bird-carrier rise, and the spring wind blows from the sea the bird-god patrols. Later centuries gradually retired him from state ceremony into the almanac and the temple mural, but the iconography never drifted: green plumage, grave face, two dragons — the spring of the world, riding to work.
