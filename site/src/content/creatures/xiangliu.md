## A Minister of the Flood God

Xiangliu's only recorded rank is a bureaucratic one: it is "a minister of Gong Gong" (共工之臣). Gong Gong — the water god who, in the *Huainanzi* (c. 139 BCE), fought Zhuanxu for the throne of Heaven and in defeat butted Mount Buzhou until the pillar of the sky snapped — fields in this creature something worse than a predator: a courtier of catastrophe. The Classic of Regions Beyond the Seas gives it nine heads that "feed from nine mountains," and the parallel passage of the Great Wilderness adds that those nine heads sit on a single serpent body "coiled in a ring upon itself," grazing the nine lands. One terse line of the original is the only portrait we get: nine heads with human faces, a snake's body, and green (九首人面，蛇身而青).

## The Blood That Ruined the Land

Yu the Great killed Xiangliu while taming the floods — and that is where the story starts, not ends. The blood of the slain monster was itself the final weapon: where it soaked the soil, the five grains would not grow; the Great Wilderness version says the stench was such that "no grain would grow, the ground seeped with water, and it could not be lived upon." Yu excavated the poisoned ground — "three times dug, three times slumped back" (三仞三沮); commentators still argue whether 仞 means to dig or to bank up, but either way the earth would not stay healed. His solution was monumental rather than agricultural: he raised the Terraces of the Many Emperors (众帝之台) over the corpse. A separate chapter of the classic lists square paired altars of Yao, of Ku, of Danzhu, and of Shun northeast of Kunlun — the standard identification for Yu's memorial. The neighboring passage adds a detail that still startles: archers dare not aim north, for fear of Gong Gong's terrace, and a tiger-striped snake crouches at each of its corners, head turned south.

## Two Tellings, One Monster

Honesty matters here: the classic preserves two versions, and they do not match perfectly. The northern-seas chapter calls the creature Xiangliu (相柳) and says it "fed from nine mountains"; the Great Wilderness chapter calls it Xiangyou (相繇), describes it coiled in a ring, makes its wetlands "not pungent but bitter," and ends with Yu digging a pool and the "many lords" raising terraces from it. The fourth-century annotator Guo Pu already pointed readers of the second passage back to the first, and the modern standard commentary of Yuan Ke treats them as one figure — a view most scholars share. The Great Wilderness chapters are generally dated later, so the Xiangyou telling is best read as an expanded retelling of the older Xiangliu passage. What neither version says is equally worth noting: no regrowing heads, no fiery breath. The nine-headed serpent is native to China.

## China's Answer to the Hydra

Western readers will reach for the comparison, and it is a fair one — nine heads, a serpent's body, a hero's blade. But the morals diverge. The Lernaean Hydra is killed by strategy: cauterize the necks or the heads multiply. Xiangliu is killed outright, and the danger it poses outlives its own death, seeping up through the soil; where Hercules wins a trophy, Yu wins a construction problem — drained marshes, collapsed excavations, and finally a platform of sovereignty raised over poisoned ground. In Chinese flood mythology the enemy is the ruined land itself, and the weapon is engineering. The comparison now runs in both directions: the nine-headed *xionghui* of the *Chu Ci*'s "Heavenly Questions" shows the type circulating by the Warring States period, and in contemporary culture Xiangliu has become the default "Chinese Hydra" — a tragic, beloved nine-headed general in Tong Hua's novel and the 2023 drama *Lost You Forever* (长相思), a Shanhaijing-themed skin line for Sima Yi in *Honor of Kings* (2025), and a fixture of online "Hydra vs. Xiangliu" myth debates.
