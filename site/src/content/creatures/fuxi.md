## The First Sovereign

Fuxi precedes history. The Chinese historical tradition places him at the beginning of civilization — before the Yellow Emperor, before Yao and Shun, before the founding of any dynasty — as one of the Three Sovereigns (三皇) whose reigns are measured not in years but in ages. His immediate successor is Shennong, the Divine Farmer; before him, there is nothing but Nüwa's act of creation. Fuxi is the moment when humanity, already bodied, first acquired tools for understanding.

## The Eight Trigrams

The *Yijing*'s commentary on the lower appendix describes Fuxi's greatest achievement without drama:

> In ancient times when Bao Xi ruled the world, he looked upward to observe the patterns in heaven and downward to observe the laws on earth. He observed the markings of birds and beasts and the fit of the earth, took examples from himself and from objects afar. In this way he began to create the Eight Trigrams, to communicate the virtue of the spirit-bright, and to classify the nature of all things.

The Eight Trigrams (八卦 bāguà) are eight combinations of three broken and unbroken lines. Each represents a natural phenomenon: heaven, earth, water, fire, thunder, wind, mountain, lake. Fuxi did not invent these phenomena; he invented the *notation* — the compressed symbolic code that allows a human to read the state of the cosmos the way a navigator reads the sky. Doubled, the trigrams become the sixty-four hexagrams of the *Yijing*, still consulted by hundreds of millions of people. The I Ching's enduring global influence is Fuxi's inheritance.

## The Net and What It Means

The *Fengsu Tongyi* credits Fuxi with inventing the knotted net for fishing and hunting. This is a less dramatic origin story than the trigrams, but it carries the same structural logic: a net is a pattern imposed on formlessness. The sea is chaos; the net gives it a grid. Whatever falls through the grid is not yet catchable — but everything that enters the grid is ordered, named, and catchable. The trigrams do to change what the net does to water.

## Fuxi and Nüwa

The pairing of Fuxi and Nüwa — brother-sister, husband-wife, or simply the two primal forces — is one of the most enduring images in Chinese mythology. Han dynasty tomb murals from Shandong, Xinjiang, and elsewhere show them entwined at the tail, their upper bodies human and upright, their lower bodies a single serpentine coil. Fuxi holds a carpenter's square (矩, representing measurement and the earth); Nüwa holds a compass (规, representing curves and heaven). The tools are the cosmological key: heaven is round, earth is square — the fundamental premise of Chinese cosmology, held in the hands of the two figures who made both.

The coiled lower body has attracted modern attention for its resemblance to a double helix, though the structural parallel is coincidental — the entwined serpents represent the union of complementary principles (yin and yang, creation and order), not molecular biology.
