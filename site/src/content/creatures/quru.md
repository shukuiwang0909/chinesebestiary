## Three Feet

The number three in pre-Qin mythology is not incidental. Two is normal: two wings, two legs, two eyes, the bilateral symmetry of living creatures. Three disrupts that symmetry and signals a nature outside the ordinary. The most famous three-legged creature in Chinese mythology is the sun-crow (三足乌), the bird that lives inside the sun — its three feet are the mark of its solar, divine nature. The Quru, with its three feet on a heron's body, belongs to this iconographic lineage: a bird that has one more leg than a bird should have, which is to say, a bird that is more than a bird.

The specific anatomy matters. The Quru is shaped like a 鵁 (*jiāo*) — a type of heron or egret, a long-legged wading bird of rivers and wetlands. Mount Daoguo, where it is found, is located in the southern mountains now associated with Guangdong — a coastal, riverine landscape where herons and egrets are common birds. The Quru takes the familiar form of the most ordinary large water bird of that environment, and gives it one extra leg.

## Human Face

The *Shanhaijing*'s hybrid logic runs on a consistent grammar: take a recognizable animal form, add a human face or a human body element, and the result is a creature that exists in the space between animal and human. The Quru has a bird's body and a human face. This makes it fundamentally different from an ordinary bird not in what it can *do* (no special abilities are listed) but in what it *is*: a creature that meets your gaze with the eyes of a person.

Ming dynasty illustrators understood this instinctively. The Jiang Yinghao woodblock shows the Quru with a bird's body rendered faithfully in heron proportions, and a face that is straightforwardly, almost comically human — a full-fronted person's face set at the top of the long bird's neck. The effect is exactly right: not terrifying, but wrong in a way that is difficult to look away from. The bird is watching you with the face of a person who knows what you are.

## Self-Named

"Its cry is its own name" (其鸣自号也) — the same formula used for the Yingyu among fish. A creature whose call is the sound of its own name has completed a kind of self-knowledge that most animals lack: it exists so completely as what it is that its voice has become its identity. Moving through the southern wetlands, the Quru calls and the air fills with the sound of its name. Every call is a declaration: *I am here. I am what I am.*
