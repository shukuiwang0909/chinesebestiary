## Ten Gods from Her Intestines

The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* mentions Nüwa exactly once, and the mention is among the strangest in the entire Classic: ten spirits called the "intestines of Nüwa," dwelling squarely across the roadway of the wilderness of Ligang (栗广之野). She is already a remote, half-effaced figure by the time this stratum was written down around the 4th century BCE — an old goddess whose original stories were slipping from memory, leaving only this bewildering epithet behind.

## The Mending of the Sky

The great rescue belongs to another book entirely. The *Huainanzi* (淮南子, presented to the Han court in 139 BCE) opens its "Discourse on the Obscure Sages" with a catastrophe: the four world-pillars shattered, the nine provinces split, unquenchable fire and unceasing flood. Nüwa's four counter-measures answer in order — five-colored stones to patch the sky, a giant turtle's legs to re-erect the pillars, a black dragon slain to save Ji province (冀州, the old heartland around the middle Yellow River), and reed ash heaped to stem the waters. A later annotator insists that the rulers of men afterward copied her deeds. The 1st-century CE skeptic Wang Chong already cites this account, so the myth was circulating early.

## Shaping Humanity from Earth

Somewhere beneath the cosmic repair sits a quieter, more intimate creation. The *Fengsu Tongyi* (风俗通义) by Ying Shao (late Eastern Han) preserves the tradition that Nüwa shaped the first people from yellow earth, kneading each one in her own image; her strength ran short, so she dragged a cord through the mud and shook off the drops, and from those splashings came the thronging multitude. A much-quoted coda divides the rich from the humble by which method made them — a social satire that is now generally regarded as a later commentator's embroidery. The original is lost and the passage survives only in quotation, so the wording above is a summary, not a quotation, of the tradition.

## The Serpent Tail

The Nüwa everyone pictures — half woman, half snake — is barely in the early texts at all: the Classic calls her nothing more than a spirit (神), and even the *Huainanzi* repairs the sky without describing her body. It is Han dynasty tomb art that fixes her canonical form: pictorial stones from Shandong and Henan show her snake-tail braided together with her brother Fuxi's, the two of them holding the compass and the square, the round heaven and the level earth. The Tang miscellany *Duyizhi* (独异志) records the matching story — Nüwa and Fuxi as the last beings after a great flood, brother and sister who married to repopulate the world. Fuxi rules heaven with the compass; Nüwa rules earth with the square; between them the world is set square and true. Later ages raised her to Wahuang (娲皇), the August Sovereign, and her temples still cluster across northern China — none grander than the temple city at Sheqiu in Hebei.
