## The Animal That Speaks

The *Book of Rites* places the Xingxing in a philosophical category alongside the parrot: "The Xingxing can speak, yet remains a beast." The sentence is a boundary marker. Language, the text implies, is not sufficient for humanity. Having words is not having personhood. The Xingxing and the parrot are the test cases — creatures with enough linguistic capacity to rattle the category, not enough to cross it.

But the *Shanhaijing* is not interested in philosophy. It wants you to know that the Xingxing has white ears, moves on four legs but can run on two, and that eating it makes you fast. The text is a pharmacopoeia as much as a bestiary, and the Xingxing's entry ends the way most entries end: a note on what consuming it does to you.

## The Name

The Xingxing knows your name before you speak it. This is the folk-tale tradition's central elaboration on the Shanhaijing's brief entry, and it is the most humanizing thing about the creature — and the most dangerous. The folk version of the story, preserved in various later texts, runs as follows:

A merchant travels near the southern mountains where the Xingxing lives. Before he gets close, he hears the creature call his name. He stops. He hears other names — the names of his traveling companions, of his family, of people he knows. The Xingxing has been watching and knows them all.

The smart merchant sets out wine and sandals at the forest edge, then hides. The Xingxing arrives, sees the wine, and says: "This is a trap — humans use wine and sandals to catch us." Then it drinks anyway, because it knows the past and not the future. The merchant captures it.

The creature's knowledge is perfect in one direction. It knows what has been — who has come before, what names have been borne. It cannot know what will come — the trap set, the wine prepared, the moment of capture. The Xingxing is a figure of tragic intelligence: knowing too much about humans to be surprised by their tricks, but not enough about time to avoid them.

## The Modern Word

The modern Chinese word 猩猩 (xīng xīng) refers to orangutans and chimpanzees — a category of great apes that arrived in Chinese zoological knowledge through Silk Road trade and later colonial-era natural history. The name transfer is semantically clean: the *Shanhaijing*'s humanoid speaking ape and the actual great apes of Southeast Asia share the property that unsettled the *Book of Rites* — they are close enough to be uncanny, far enough to remain animals.
