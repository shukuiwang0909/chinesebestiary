## Not in the Classic of Mountains and Seas

A note of honesty for a bestiary built on that classic: the qilin is not in it. The beast enters the record earlier and calmer — as a compliment in an ode of the *Book of Poetry*, as one line in the *Spring and Autumn Annals* for 481 BCE, and as a member of the Four Numina in the *Book of Rites*. Every later extravagance grows from these sober seeds.

## The Beast That Would Not Harm

Han exegetes built the qilin's character from the ode: the hoof that will not tread living grass, the brow that will not gore, the horn sheathed in flesh (He Xiu: "bearing arms yet harming none"). Lu Ji adds that it never herds or pairs — a hermit among beasts, appearing only for a ruler of true benevolence.

## The Capture That Closed an Era

In 481 BCE, hunters of Lu took a strange beast in the western hunt. Confucius looked and named it: "It is a lin." Knowing it had come out of season, into a doomed age, he wept — "My way is at an end" — and laid down his brush. The *Spring and Autumn Annals* ends with that line.

## From Giraffe to Global Icon

In 1414 a giraffe arrived from Bengal as tribute, and the Ming court joyfully received it as a living qilin. The name traveled to Japan as *kirin* — now a famous trademark and a fixture of video games — while in the West the qilin circulates as the "Chinese unicorn": benevolence and good fortune given hooves and a single gentle horn.
