## The Dragon That Stopped the Rain

The *Great Wilderness: East* plants a paradox at the northeast corner of the world. Yinglong dwelt at the southern extreme — and he slew Chiyou and Kuafu, and afterward could not ascend again to heaven. For this, droughts recurred below. A rain-dragon caused droughts: the two most consequential kills in mythic history — the war-god rebel Chiyou and Kuafu the giant who chased the sun — spent his passport back to the sky. The Classic even records the fix. When drought came, the people fashioned images in Yinglong's likeness, and then great rain was obtained. It is among the oldest descriptions of a rain-prayer rite anywhere in Chinese writing: effigy, petition, answer.

## Wings on a Dragon

Ying means to answer, to respond — the dragon that answers heaven's summons. His answering organ is the pair of great wings no other dragon in the Classic carries: the older Chinese dragon flies by magic, by storm, by will; Yinglong flies the way an obligation is fulfilled, visibly and on purpose. Later ages found the wings so defining that 'yinglong' became the ordinary literary word for a winged dragon of any kind, and painters gave the Ming encyclopedia its standard portrait of him — horned, clawed, and shouldered with pinions, coiling through cloud.

## River-Drawer of the Great Flood

His second career began in a commentary. The *Heavenly Questions* of the *Songs of Chu* asks, tersely, "What did Yinglong draw?" — and Wang Yi, annotating in the Han dynasty, supplies the scene: when Yu the Great tamed the flood, Yinglong went before him, and where the dragon's tail swept across the earth, river channels opened, and the waters were led away. It made him the engineer of China's second great waters: not the destroyer of Gonggong's smashed-pillar deluge, but the drainer of Yu's ordered one. The two flood stories divide the labor of water itself — rage and remedy.

## Southbound

The *Great Wilderness: North* closes the file with the matching notice: Yinglong had slain Chiyou, and had also slain Kuafu; thereupon he went away to dwell in the south — and for this the south receives much rain. The two passages bracket the whole map of the Classic: a war-dragon posted to the southern marches, raining on the rice country ever after. Later tradition settled him comfortably into the lineage of the dragon kings who answer a village's prayer with a storm, and the rain-effigies of the old rite became, in the end, the dragons painted on village rain altars to this day.
