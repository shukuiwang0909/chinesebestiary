## The Man-Faced Beast of Mount Gouwu

The northern chapters of the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* (compiled roughly 4th–1st century BCE) record a creature called the Paoxiao (狍鸮) on Mount Gouwu: the body of a ram wearing a human face, tiger's teeth, human claws — and, most unnervingly, its eyes set in the hollows of its armpits. Its cry is the wail of a human infant, the same lure the nine-tailed fox sets in the eastern mountains; anyone who follows the sound into the hills does not come back. The fourth-century commentator Guo Pu was the first to declare that this paoxiao was none other than the Taotie of the chronicles, and his identification stuck.

## From Monster to Moral Verdict

By the 7th century BCE the name had escaped the mountains. The *Zuo Zhuan* chronicle fastens Taotie onto the gluttonous son of the Jinyun clan — a man so greedy for food, bribes, and hoarded wealth that he shared nothing with widows or orphans — and the label hardened into the standard word for rapacious appetite. Later tradition folded him into the Four Perils (四凶): [Qiongqi](/creatures/qiongqi), [Hundun](/creatures/hundun), and [Taowu](/creatures/taowu), each a human vice given a monster's body.

## The Face That Ate the Bronze Age

The taotie's face was famous before the chronicle was ever written. From the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE), the great ritual ding and zun vessels — most spectacularly the Houmuwu Ding — were wrapped in the horned, fanged 'taotie mask,' split in perfect symmetry across the vessel's flanks. The *Lüshi Chunqiu* (3rd century BCE) supplies the gloss: the bronze taotie has a head but no body, because in devouring people it finally consumed itself. The warning softened over the centuries — modern Chinese now uses 'taotie feast' (饕餮盛宴) to praise an extravagant banquet and 'laotao' (老饕) to honor a devoted gourmet.

## The Monster Hollywood Met

For most Western viewers, one film defines the beast: Zhang Yimou's *The Great Wall* (2016), in which swarm-minded taotie pour out of the mountains every sixty years to devour everything in their path. The creature design keeps the antique anatomy — eyes clustered on the shoulders, a body low and ravenous — and scales it into a blockbuster horde.

## Sanxingdui: A Different Bronze Tradition

After Sanxingdui's discovery in 1986, many popular accounts described its bronze masks as "taotie." The comparison is understandable but imprecise. The taotie mask convention — a bilateral split face without a lower jaw, rendered in shallow surface relief — is specific to the Shang and early Zhou Central Plains tradition. The Sanxingdui bronze faces are three-dimensional, full-jawed, and naturalistic in a way the taotie mask is not. The most famous Sanxingdui artifact, the protruding-eye mask (纵目铜面具), has cylindrical eyes extending 16 cm forward — a feature with no parallel in any taotie-tradition vessel. Both traditions used frontally depicted faces on sacred objects, but they did so in ways that reflect distinct mythological vocabularies. The Sanxingdui face tradition is now linked by most scholars to the Shu kingdom's own cosmology, including the legendary figure [Cancong](/creatures/cancong/), whose "vertical eyes" were described in texts a millennium after the masks were made. For the full comparison, see the [Sanxingdui article](/articles/sanxingdui-chinese-mythology/).
