id: qilin
claim: |
  The qilin, the theory says, was a giraffe. Not a vague resemblance — a documented identification. In 1414, the Yongle Emperor's treasure fleets brought a giraffe from Bengal to the Ming court, and the courtiers hailed it as a qilin, the supreme omen-beast whose appearance certifies a ruler's virtue. Modern commentators conclude the case is closed: the qilin myth was a giraffe all along, or at least became one the moment the real animal walked into Nanjing.
evidence: |
  The 1414 event is solid history. The giraffe, a gift from the King of Bengal, was received as a qilin by the Ming court and painted by court artists; contemporary poems celebrating the auspicious arrival survive. The circumstantial fit is real, too: the classical qilin is described as having a deer's body, hooves, an ox's tail, a fleshy horn, and a gentle nature that does not tread on living grass — a description a giraffe satisfies surprisingly well. To this day, the word for giraffe in Korean and Japanese (girin, kirin) is the word for qilin, which seems to seal the argument.
scholars_say: |
  Historians agree on the event and dispute the conclusion. The qilin was already a thousand years old when the giraffe arrived: it appears in the Spring and Autumn Annals (the capture of a lin in 481 BCE, which Confucius reportedly mourned), in Han dynasty omen-lore, and in centuries of court art that looks nothing like a giraffe — a scaled, antlered, lion-maned chimera. What happened in 1414 was not a zoological identification but a political act: presenting the exotic gift as a qilin flattered the Yongle Emperor's virtue at exactly the moment his legitimacy needed heaven's endorsement. The identification ran from politics to animal, not from animal to myth. The modern East Asian usage of "kirin/girin" for giraffe is the afterlife of that Ming court ceremony — a loan, not a fossil of the original meaning.

The giraffe episode is the rare "origin theory" where the documented event is true — and still doesn't explain the myth. A qilin was captured, mourned by Confucius, and woven into omen-lore long before any Chinese court saw a giraffe. The 1414 ceremony is better understood as the myth absorbing a real animal than the myth being born from one.

This is the pattern to watch for in all "was X real?" debates: a myth old enough to have paperwork, meeting an animal new enough to be startling. The myth always wins the naming rights — but that is publicity, not paternity.
