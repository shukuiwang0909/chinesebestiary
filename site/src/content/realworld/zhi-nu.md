  The astronomical identification is explicit and ancient. Vega (α Lyrae) is 织女星 (Weaving Girl Star) and Altair (α Aquilae) is 牛郎星 (Cowherd Star) in Chinese astronomy — these names appear in star catalogs from at least the Han dynasty and are confirmed in the Mawangdui astronomical silk texts (c. 168 BCE). In the Chinese lunar mansion system, 女 (Woman, the 10th mansion) and 牛 (Ox, the 9th mansion) are adjacent mansions on either side of the Milky Way. On summer evenings from June through September, Vega and Altair are both visible overhead in the Northern Hemisphere, separated by the Milky Way (天河, Heavenly River), which runs between them. The Qixi Festival date — the seventh night of the seventh lunar month — corresponds to the period when both stars are highest and most visible.

Joseph Needham's *Science and Civilisation in China* (Vol. 3) treats the four-quadrant system and the 28 mansions as genuine positional astronomy, noting that the star names preserve observational knowledge in poetic form. The romantic story is mythology; the stellar identification is astronomy.

The Weaving Girl and Cowherd myth is unusual in Chinese mythology because its factual substrate is fully verifiable: look up on a clear summer night. Vega is one of the brightest stars visible from the Northern Hemisphere; Altair is another; the Milky Way runs between them. The separation that the myth calls unbearable is the gap between α Lyrae and α Aquilae — approximately 16 light-years at their physical positions, reduced to the width of a galaxy's plane as seen from Earth.

What the myth adds that the sky does not provide is the love story, the magpie bridge, and the one night per year. The stars do not actually approach each other on the seventh of the seventh month. But they are real, they are separated, and they have had names in Chinese astronomy for longer than almost any other named stars on Earth.
