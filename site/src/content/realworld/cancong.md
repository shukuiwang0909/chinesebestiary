id: cancong
claim: >
  The protruding-eye bronze masks of Sanxingdui — featuring cylindrical bronze eyes extending up to 16 centimeters forward from the face — represent the mythological Shu king Cancong (蚕丛), who is described in the Chronicle of the Kings of Shu as having "vertical eyes" (其目纵, qí mù zòng).
evidence: >
  The earliest textual source for Cancong's "vertical eyes" is Yang Xiong's Chronicle of the Kings of Shu (蜀王本纪, c. 1st century BCE): 蜀候蚕丛，其目纵，始称王 ("The Shu lord Cancong, his eyes vertical, was the first to be called king"). The Sanxingdui Bronze Age site (c. 1200–1000 BCE) in Guanghan, Sichuan — approximately 40 km north of Chengdu — produced numerous bronze masks with eyes that project dramatically forward on cylindrical stalks, unlike anything in the Shang or Zhou bronze traditions of the Central Plains. The most extreme example (artifact K2②：149-1) extends 16 cm forward. This artifact type has no parallel in the contemporary archaeological record outside Sichuan. The geographic and cultural overlap between the ancient Shu kingdom and the Sanxingdui site is well established — the Sanxingdui culture is now widely understood as a precursor or early phase of the Shu kingdom tradition.
scholars_say: >
  Most Chinese archaeologists (including Zhao Dianzeng, Chen De'an, and the teams of the Sanxingdui Museum) accept a "plausible connection" between the protruding-eye motif and the 纵目 description, while stopping short of identification. The gap is chronological: the Sanxingdui bronzes predate Yang Xiong's chronicle by roughly 1,000–1,200 years, which means the text may be a garbled oral memory of the masks, a parallel tradition that independently used vertical-eye imagery for divine figures, or a later legend explaining artifacts that were already mysterious. Robert Bagley (Princeton) notes that the protruding-eye masks are iconographically unique to the Shu region and likely served a ritual function — possibly representing a deity or ancestor rather than a historical king. The identification of Cancong as the specific figure depicted remains unconfirmed because Sanxingdui left no writing.
verdict: unverified
