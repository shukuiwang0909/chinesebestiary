id: zhulong
claim: |
  A popular internet theory — common in Chinese blog essays and YouTube "mysteries" videos — holds that Zhulong, the Torch Dragon of the far north, was never a deity at all. It was the aurora borealis. A traveler or tribute-bearer, the story goes, journeyed so far north that he saw the polar sky on fire, and the Shanhaijing simply wrote down his report: a red, serpentine thing that lights up the darkness.
evidence: |
  The theory's supporters marshal genuine textual details. Zhulong lives at the edge of the world, "beyond the northern seas," near Mount Zhongwei — a plausible direction for Arctic phenomena. It is red and "illuminates the nine darknesses" (照九阴), which matches the aurora's blood-red curtains. Most seductively, the text says the dragon "opens its eyes and it is day; closes them and it is night" (视为昼，瞑为夜) — which supporters read as a garbled account of the polar day-night cycle, where the sun neither rises nor sets for months. Every element of the description, they argue, has a natural counterpart in the far north.
scholars_say: |
  Scholars point out that the theory reads the passage in exactly the wrong direction. The Shanhaijing's far north is a cosmographic address, not a geographic one — the same chapters place physically impossible beings at every point of the compass. Zhulong's name is literal: 烛龙, "Torch Dragon," and the text says it carries a light (衔烛) — it is a cosmic lamp-keeper, part of a system of directional deities that includes the sun-mother Xihe and the moon, not a weather report. The eye-opening day-night formula is a sovereignty-over-time motif that appears in the Huainanzi's cosmology as well, applied to divine beings generally. No pre-modern commentator, from Guo Pu in the 4th century onward, ever read the passage as a natural phenomenon. And the fatal detail is the body: the text gives Zhulong a human face on a serpent's body and describes its breathing, sleep, and waking — anatomy, not atmosphere. Auroras do not sleep.
verdict: dubious

The aurora theory is a beautiful example of a modern habit: assuming ancient people were bad scientists rather than good mythologists. Zhulong belongs to a family of light-bearing gods built to explain why night follows day — the question the aurora theory asks ("what did they see?") is simply not the question the text is answering ("who governs the darkness?").

The theory survives because it flatters us: it makes the Shanhaijing a lost field report instead of a cosmology, and makes us the detectives who cracked it. The flat, descriptive style of the bestiary invites exactly this misreading — which is why the same style has produced a hundred similar theories, most of which collapse on the same detail: the creature has a face.
