id: kun-peng
claim: |
  Kun-peng, the colossal fish that transforms into a colossal bird in the opening of the Zhuangzi, is often explained as a whale — or rather, as two whale stories folded together. Blog posts and nature documentaries alike suggest that ancient sailors saw a whale's back ("several thousand li across," as the text boasts), mistook its flukes for wings as it breached, and the transformation myth was born.
evidence: |
  The surface logic is attractive. Kun (鲲) is a fish so vast that no one knows how many thousand li it measures; when it changes into the peng bird, its wings "are like clouds hanging from the sky." Whales are the only real animals that approach this scale, they do launch themselves skyward, and ancient Chinese coastal communities certainly knew of whales — stranded ones were recorded as sea monsters in local gazetteers. The "whale breach = bird transformation" reading gives the myth a tidy naturalistic origin.
scholars_say: |
  Scholars object on the level of genre. The Zhuangzi is philosophy, not bestiary: the Kun-peng passage is the opening parable of "Free and Easy Wandering," a deliberate, self-aware exercise in scale meant to dwarf the small-minded. Zhuangzi tells us he is inventing — the passage cites a lost book of wonders (the Qi Xie) as a mock citation, a wink at the reader. The whale reading also fails on detail: the peng flies ninety thousand li upward to ride the wind to the southern darkness, a trajectory no breach explains. Most importantly, the parable's point depends on impossibility — if Kun-peng were just a big real animal, the fable about the limits of small perspectives would collapse into a fish story. Later literature kept both halves magical: the Kun-peng became poetry's standard emblem of ambition, not of zoology.
verdict: unlikely

The whale theory mistakes the Zhuangzi for the Shanhaijing. A bestiary entry can, in principle, hide a real animal; a philosophical parable cannot, because its exaggeration is the argument. Zhuangzi needs a creature too big for any net, any pond, any horizon — and a whale, however grand, fits in the ocean.

Still, the theory tells us something true about reading: given a text that measures an animal in thousands of li, we instinctively reach for the biggest thing in nature. The ancients reached in the opposite direction — given the biggest thing in nature, they invented a text to outgrow it.
