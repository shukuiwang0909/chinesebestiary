  The ancient pixiu is a war guardian and celestial beast — its earliest function is protective and martial, not financial. Han dynasty bronze pixiu and tomb figures serve the same apotropaic role as the bixie (辟邪): they ward off evil forces and protect the dead, not attract cash. The "no anus" detail and the wealth-magnet function are absent from the Shanhaijing and appear in medieval and late imperial sources, where popular religion gradually grafted economic functions onto guardian figures. Art historian Wu Hung's work on Han mortuary art treats pixiu firmly in the protective category; the financial overlay is a post-Tang development at the earliest. The Shanhaijing is silent on any connection to money.

The feng shui pixiu is a descendant of the Shanhaijing's beast in the way that a corporate logo is a descendant of the original animal: the shape is recognizable; the function has been entirely redesigned. There is no ancient text that instructs you to place a pixiu on your desk to accumulate wealth. There are many ancient texts that describe the pixiu as a fierce divine predator that protects borders, wards off evil, and belongs on battle standards.

The modern talisman industry is not wrong that the pixiu is a powerful figure in Chinese tradition. It is wrong about what that power originally was for.
