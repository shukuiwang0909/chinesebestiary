## The Shepherdess by the Jing River

The *Tale of Liu Yi* (柳毅传), written by Li Chaowei in the Tang dynasty, is the story that taught China what a dragon palace looks like. It opens with failure: Liu Yi, a scholar from Hunan, has just flunked the imperial examination in the capital and is riding home in disgrace. On the banks of the Jing River he passes a woman herding sheep — and she is weeping so hard the landscape seems to bend around her.

She is no shepherdess. She is the youngest daughter of the **Dragon King of Dongting Lake** (洞庭龙君), married off to the son of the Jing River dragon — a husband who grew bored of her, favored lesser wives, and, when she complained to her in-laws, was punished by being sent out to the shore to herd sheep in the rain. The dragons' domestic cruelty is rendered with a Tang novelist's eye: the sheep are not even sheep, but rain-beasts, and when she weeps, the sky weeps with her.

She asks Liu Yi for one thing — not rescue, not revenge, but a letter:

> 妾，洞庭龙君小女也。…… 闻君将还吴，密通洞庭，或以尺书寄托侍者，未卜将以为可乎？
>
> "I am the youngest daughter of the Dragon Lord of Dongting… I hear you are returning south, by way of my home. If I entrusted a letter to you — could I dare hope you would carry it?"

He could. That is the entire hinge of the story: a failed scholar, good for nothing in the capital's eyes, turns out to be good for the one thing that matters — he says yes, and means it.

## The Palace Beneath the Lake

Liu Yi travels to Dongting Lake, strikes the sacred tangerine tree on the isle of Junshan three times as instructed, and a warrior parts the waters. The palace beneath is everything later dragon imagery would copy: "columns of white jade, walls of crystal, windows of green glass" — and upon its throne, the Dragon King, in black silk, reading his daughter's letter with the stillness of a man absorbing catastrophe.

The stillness lasts one scene. Then the letter reaches the harem, the palace erupts in lamentation, and Liu Yi learns about the king's younger brother — **Qiantangjun** (钱塘君), lord of the Qiantang River, currently demoted and confined to the palace for flooding the world in a fit of temper. When Qiantangjun asks what the matter is, and is told, the text delivers its most famous sentence:

> 语未毕，而大声忽发，天坼地裂，宫殿摆簸，云烟沸涌。俄有赤龙长千余丈，电目血舌，朱鳞火鬣，项掣金锁，锁牵玉柱，千雷万霆，激绕其身。…… 掣开其锁，腾空而去。
>
> Before the words were finished, a great sound burst forth — heaven split and earth cracked, the palace halls swayed, clouds and smoke boiled up. In an instant there appeared a red dragon over a thousand zhang long: lightning eyes, a bloody tongue, scarlet scales, a mane of fire, a golden chain around its neck, the chain tethered to a pillar of jade, a thousand thunderclaps winding about its body. … It snapped the lock and hurled itself into the sky.

He flies to the Jing River, destroys the palace there, and eats the cruel husband. Whole. The narrative pauses only to note how quickly it happened — Qiantangjun returns before the feast has cooled, the princess restored, and politely reports the casualty figures to his brother.

## What the Story Made

The tale ends slowly, as Tang tales do: Liu Yi refuses the princess's hand twice (the author is punctilious about his hero's motives — he came to deliver a letter, not to marry into a lake), and marries her years later anyway, when both have shed their first marriages and their mortal disguises. He becomes, by the final line, something like an immortal.

What it made is more durable than its ending. Every later Dragon King — the *Fengshen Yanyi*'s Ao Guang, the Dragon Kings of the Four Seas in *Journey to the West*, the dragon kings of a thousand temple murals — descends from the household the *Tale of Liu Yi* built: the jade-columned palace, the sheep that are rain, the uncle who is weather wearing a name. When the bestiary's [Dragon King entry](/creatures/long-wang/) describes a god of rain and rivers, this story is where the god acquired a family, an address, and a temper.
