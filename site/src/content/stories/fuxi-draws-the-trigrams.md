## Before the Book

The *Yijing* — the I Ching, the Book of Changes — is one of the most consulted texts in human history. It has been used for divination in China for three thousand years. It has been commented on by Confucius (or attributed to him), translated into every major language, analyzed by Jung, and applied to decision-making in everything from business strategy to personal relationships. Its influence on East Asian philosophy is deeper than any single summary can capture.

At the beginning of the *Yijing*'s appendix on the lower section (系辞下), there is a sentence that explains where the book came from:

> 古者包牺氏之王天下也，仰则观象于天，俯则观法于地，观鸟兽之文与地之宜，近取诸身，远取诸物，于是始作八卦。
>
> "In ancient times when Bao Xi ruled the world, he looked upward to observe the patterns in heaven and downward to observe the laws on earth. He observed the markings of birds and beasts and the fit of the earth, took examples from himself and from objects afar. In this way he began to create the Eight Trigrams."

Four things to observe: heaven above, earth below, the markings of living creatures, and the patterns on nearby objects. Then one synthesis: eight symbols. Fuxi is the person who held all four sources of observation together long enough to find the pattern they share.

## The Method

The method is inductive. Fuxi did not receive the trigrams from a deity or discover them in a sacred text. He derived them from sustained observation — of the sky's movements, the earth's structure, the markings on bird feathers and animal hides, and the forms of the objects around him (including his own body).

What was he looking for? The *Xici* is specific: he was looking for *patterns* (象, *xiàng*). Not objects, not events, but the formal relationships that recur across different domains. The sky has a pattern; the earth has a pattern; the tiger's stripes have a pattern; the human body has a pattern. Fuxi was not cataloguing things — he was looking for what the things share at a structural level.

The Eight Trigrams are what he found: eight combinations of three lines, where each line is either unbroken (—, yang) or broken (– –, yin). Eight combinations cover all the possibilities for three binary choices. Each combination corresponds to a natural phenomenon:

☰ Heaven (three unbroken lines): pure yang, the father  
☷ Earth (three broken lines): pure yin, the mother  
☳ Thunder: yang breaking below two yin  
☴ Wind: yin penetrating below two yang  
☵ Water: yang enclosed between two yin  
☲ Fire: yin enclosed between two yang  
☶ Mountain: yang resting above two yin  
☱ Lake: yin open above two yang  

These eight, doubled into pairs, become the sixty-four hexagrams of the *Yijing*. The binary logic of yin and yang, organized into three-line symbols and then doubled: sixty-four states covering all the conditions of change.

Leibniz, reading a Jesuit account of the hexagrams in 1701, recognized the sixty-four as a binary numeral system — every number from 0 to 63 encoded in a sequence of broken and unbroken lines. The structural identity between Fuxi's cosmological notation and binary arithmetic is not coincidental: both are derived from the same underlying logic of two states, three positions, recursive combination. Fuxi was not doing mathematics. He was doing something more fundamental: finding the minimal notation system capable of describing all change.

## The Net

The trigrams are the famous invention. The *Fengsu Tongyi* records another one, less celebrated, with the same structural logic:

> 结网罟，以教佃渔。
>
> "He knotted nets to teach hunting and fishing."

A net is also a pattern imposed on formlessness. The sea is undifferentiated — fish could be anywhere. A net divides the sea into cells. Each cell either contains a fish or does not. The net is a binary grid applied to continuous water: a physical version of the same yin/yang, full/empty logic that the trigrams apply to change.

Fuxi did not invent the net to catch fish. Or rather: he invented the net to catch fish, but the intellectual move is the same as the trigrams. The world is continuous and undifferentiated; the pattern-maker divides it into cells; the cells allow something to be caught that could not be caught without the grid. The trigrams do to change what the net does to the sea.

This is what the *Xici* means by "classify the nature of all things" (以类万物之情). Classification is not naming — it is grid-making. You impose a structure, and suddenly things that were floating freely in an undifferentiated medium become catchable.

## Fuxi and Nüwa

The Han dynasty tomb murals from Shandong, Xinjiang, and elsewhere show Fuxi and Nüwa entwined at the tail: two serpentine figures, their upper bodies human and upright, their lower bodies coiled together into a single spiral. He holds a carpenter's square (矩 *jǔ*); she holds a compass (规 *guī*). He measures the straight lines; she draws the curves. Heaven is round; earth is square. She holds the round tool; he holds the square one.

This seems backward until you think about it: Fuxi, the male sovereign, holds the square (earth); Nüwa, the female creator, holds the compass (heaven). The assignment is deliberately inverted — the male principle does not automatically equal yang/heaven, nor the female yin/earth. Each holds the other's emblem. They are each other's complement, not each other's mirror.

Nüwa made humans from clay. She populated the world. Fuxi gave the world's population tools for understanding what they were in. Without Nüwa's act, there is no one to use the trigrams. Without Fuxi's act, the humans Nüwa made have no framework for making sense of the changes around them. The two acts are prerequisites for each other: bodies and tools, simultaneously necessary.

## What Remains

The trigrams are inscribed on the national flag of South Korea. They are tattooed on hundreds of thousands of people worldwide who may not know their origin. They appear on protective talismans, martial arts school insignia, and the covers of I Ching editions in forty languages. Feng shui practitioners use them to orient buildings. Software engineers recognize in them the structure of binary code.

Fuxi did not know any of this. He watched birds and noticed their markings. He watched the sky and noticed its patterns. He made eight symbols. The *Yijing* says the symbols communicate "the virtue of the spirit-bright and classify the nature of all things."

They are still doing both.
