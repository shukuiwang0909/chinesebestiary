## Four Words

The *Spring and Autumn Annals* — the terse court chronicle of the state of Lu, tradition's own textbook — spends four characters on it, in its final year:

> 十有四年，春，西狩获麟。
>
> "In the fourteenth year, in spring, they hunted in the west and captured a lin."

A *lin* (麟) — the qilin. That is the whole entry. No celebration, no description. A chronicle that records eclipses and assassinations in the same flat tone records the capture of the omen-beast the same way, and then — two years of thin entries later — simply stops. The Annals end here, mid-reign, mid-sentence, with the duke still alive. Tradition has always read the four words as the book's true ending: the chronicle had nothing left to say.

## The Commentary Fills the Silence

The *Gongyang Commentary* (公羊传), written to explain exactly such silences, tells the rest. The hunters at Daye caught something none of them could identify — a beast with a deer's body, an ox's tail, a single horn. The men who could not name it were ashamed, and they threw the carcass aside. Someone thought to fetch Confucius.

Confucius looked at it and knew it at once — and wept. The commentary gives him the outburst that founded the qilin's meaning for the next two and a half thousand years:

> 麟者，仁兽也。有王者则至，无王者则不至。
>
> "The lin is a beast of benevolence. When a true king rules, it appears; when there is no true king, it does not come."

And then, the commentary says, the sage's despair broke through: 吾道穷矣 — "My way is at an end." A qilin had come to an age with no king to deserve it, and the age had answered with hunting spears. The omen arrived; the world it was meant for did not exist. Confucius understood the beast, understood the times, and understood what the carcass in the ditch meant about the distance between them. Within two years he was dead, and the Annals he had compiled — tradition insists — closed with the hunt.

## What the Story Made

This is the hinge the qilin turns on ever after. Because of this story, the qilin is not merely lucky: it is the universe's letter of reference, delivered only to the deserving — and its misdelivery is the saddest event in the classics. Every later qilin sighting is read against 481 BCE: is the reign worthy? Is the beast safe? When courtiers memorialized qilin appearances to flatter emperors, the ghost of the dead lin of Daye stood behind the flattery, accusing it.

The bestiary's [Qilin entry](/creatures/qilin/) carries the creature's description and its place among the Four Divine. This story is the reason the description matters: a beast whose appearance is a judgment cannot be looked at idly. Confucius knew what he was looking at. That is why he wept.
