## The Encounter on the Eastern Shore

The legend, as the later compendia tell it (the *Xuanyuan Benji* tradition of the Yellow Emperor's reign), is a single quiet scene. The Yellow Emperor — the culture hero who has already given his people the compass, the calendar, and the writing of records — is touring the eastern marches of the world, along the sea. There he meets a beast: white, leonine, speaking perfect human speech.

It is **Bai Ze** (白泽), and it knows everything. Not in the way oracles know the future — in the way a library knows its shelves. The beast can name every ghost, goblin, mountain-spirit and water-imp under heaven: 11,520 kinds of them, their true names, their true forms, and — this is the point — the method for dealing with each. In demon-lore, to know a thing's true name is to have power over it. Bai Ze has all the names.

The Emperor does what an emperor does: he commissions the reference work. The beast dictates; the scribes record; the result is the *Baize Tu* (白泽图), the "Diagrams of Bai Ze" — the world's first demonography, part bestiary, part field manual, issued to the realm so that no traveler need meet a demon unprepared.

## The Book That Outlived Itself

The *Baize Tu* is lost — no complete copy survives, only fragments found among the Dunhuang manuscripts and quotations in other books. But for roughly a thousand years it was the most practical book in China. The *Baopuzi* (抱朴子, c. 320 CE), the Daoist handbook for mountain travel, prescribes it by name: know the demons' names, carry the *Baize Tu* and the *Jiuding Ji*, and the hosts of ghosts withdraw of themselves. Households hung the diagrams by the door; travelers carried excerpts; the book's authority outlasted its text, the way a vanished lighthouse still organizes the harbor.

And the beast became its own book. Bai Ze turns up in later art as a lion-headed archive, an emblem of *knowing* — the patron of anyone whose protection comes from having read further than the danger. The bestiary you are reading stands in that line: a catalogue of true names and true forms, made so that no reader meets a creature unprepared. The [Bai Ze entry](/creatures/bai-ze/) gives the beast's own record; this story is its job description.

## Why the First Encyclopedia Was a Beast

There is a quiet joke in the legend, and the tradition seems to have intended it. The Chinese imagined that the complete catalogue of the world's monsters was not written by a scholar but *dictated by one of the monsters* — a talking beast that turned itself in, name by name, all eleven and a half thousand of its kin. Knowledge of demons, from a demon's mouth. The Yellow Emperor's genius was not to fight the bestiary but to publish it.

That is the deal the *Baize Tu* made with every household that hung it: the unknown is not banished, it is *indexed*. Once a thing has a name and an entry, the dark is just a room with the light off.
