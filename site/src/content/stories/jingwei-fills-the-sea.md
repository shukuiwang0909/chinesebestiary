## Nine Characters

The complete source passage in the Classic of Mountains and Seas is nine characters of substance:

> 炎帝之少女，名曰女娃。女娃游于东海，溺而不返，故为精卫，常衔西山之木石，以堙于东海。
>
> "The youngest daughter of Yan Di was named Nüwa. Nüwa went to play in the Eastern Sea and drowned, never to return. Thus she became the Jingwei bird, and forever carries wood and stones from the Western Mountains to fill in the Eastern Sea."

That is everything the Shanhaijing offers. No age, no season, no witnesses. The daughter went to the sea and drowned. The bird appears. The carrying begins. The economy is characteristic — the Shanhaijing does not explain transformations or console the reader. It states the outcome and trusts the strangeness to do its work.

## The Daughter of the Flame Emperor

Yan Di (炎帝), the Flame Emperor, is one of the great ancestral sovereigns of Chinese mythology — the culture hero who gave humanity fire, taught them to farm, and discovered medicinal plants by tasting every herb himself (the tradition says he had a transparent body so he could watch their effects directly). His daughter's name, Nüwa — not to be confused with the goddess Nüwa (女娲) who patched the sky — is simply "girl," the diminutive of a private grief.

She went to play in the Eastern Sea. The text does not say she fell from a boat, or that a storm came up, or that she went too far. The drowning is absolute and unexplained — the sea took her, and it did not give her back. For a daughter of the Flame Emperor, lord of fire and summer's generative warmth, the Eastern Sea is the opposite of everything she was. She died in her opposite element.

## What Became of Her

The transformation into Jingwei (精卫) is instantaneous in the text — "thus she became." The Shanhaijing describes the bird a few lines earlier:

> 有鸟焉，其状如乌，文首，白喙，赤足，名曰精卫，其鸣自詨。
>
> "There is a bird there, shaped like a crow, with a patterned head, white beak, and red feet. Its name is Jingwei. Its call sounds like its own name."

The color pattern — dark body, white beak, red feet — is both visually precise and symbolically loaded. White and red are the colors of ritual and sacrifice; the dark body is the darkness of the sea she came from. She announces herself by name with every call, as if the transformation required her to keep identifying what she had been.

## The Task That Cannot Be Finished

The task Jingwei sets herself is not expressed as revenge, not as grief, not as purpose — only as action. She carries wood and stones from the Western Mountains and drops them in the sea. This has continued since before the Zhou dynasty recorded her. The sea has not filled.

Qu Yuan's *Questions to Heaven* (天问, c. 4th century BCE) invokes her as one of a series of inexplicable cosmic injustices:

> 何往营班禄，不但还来？
>
> "Why does she go on laboring, and not simply return?"

The question is asked without expecting an answer. The questioner knows there is no answer. She does not return because she is already dead; she cannot return because what killed her is what she is trying to defeat. The task is not unfinishable because she is weak. It is unfinishable because it is structurally impossible — and she has decided to do it anyway.

## The Image That Lasted

Of all the creatures in the Shanhaijing, the Jingwei bird has had the most continuous afterlife as a moral image. Chinese literature returns to her when it needs a figure for persistence against odds that cannot be calculated. Her task does not require courage in the warrior sense — no enemy to face, no glory on offer, no audience. Just the western mountains, the eastern sea, and the crossing between them, repeated until time ends.

The 20th century found in her a revolutionary metaphor: the small, the persistent, and the determined against the immovably large. In an era when individual effort against institutional power needed a name, Jingwei offered one. The same tiny bird carrying the same pebble.
