## What the Book of Rites Said

The *Book of Rites*, in its summary of the rules of propriety, places two creatures in the same category:

> 猩猩能言，不离禽兽。
>
> "The Xingxing can speak, yet remains a beast."

The parrot is the other example. Both creatures are language-capable but not human. Having words, the text implies, is not sufficient. The Xingxing can speak but remains an animal — a statement designed to mark the boundary of personhood, to say: this is where human ends and animal begins.

But the text was written by people who had never met a Xingxing. The stories told by people who had come close to one are considerably more complicated.

## The Voice Before the Path

Every account of the Xingxing's encounter with humans begins the same way: the Xingxing speaks first.

You are walking on a path through the southern mountains. The trees are thick. You have not yet seen the creature. Before you see it, it calls your name.

Not a sound like your name. Your actual name. The name your parents gave you, the name your neighbors use, the name your creditors know. The Xingxing has been watching the path for some time. It has seen who came before you, learned what they were called, built a catalog. When you arrive, it already has you.

The *Book of Rites* says it speaks but remains a beast. The folk tradition adds: it knows what it says. The Xingxing's speech is not imitation — not the parrot's phonetic echo. It is knowledge. The ape knows your name because it has observed you, tracked you, filed you. Its speech is taxonomic. It speaks you into its catalog before you know it has noticed you.

## The Catalog

The *You Yang Za Zu* and other Tang-Song miscellany collections preserve the elaborated tradition in roughly consistent form. The Xingxing, they say:

- Knows the names of all humans who have passed through its territory
- Knows the names of the families those humans belong to
- Knows what has happened in the region — who has been there, what has been taken, what has been traded

This is the meaning of the later gloss: 猩猩知往而不知来 — "the Xingxing knows the past but not the future." Its knowledge is backward-looking and complete. It knows everything that has already happened. It cannot predict what will happen next.

## The Trap

Merchants in the southern trade routes discovered this asymmetry and turned it into a reliable method.

The method is simple. Leave wine and sandals at the edge of the Xingxing's forest. Hide. Wait.

The Xingxing arrives. It looks at the wine. It looks at the sandals.

And then it says — the accounts vary in exact phrasing but agree on the substance — something like: *"These are a trap. I know humans. They leave wine and sandals to catch us. We should not drink this wine."*

The merchants, hidden, hear this. They do not move. They wait.

The Xingxing looks at the wine again.

It drinks.

## What the Drinking Means

The most common reading is: the Xingxing has a weakness for wine, and weakness wins over knowledge. The trap works because the ape cannot resist.

But the story is stranger than a story about weakness. The Xingxing did not fail to recognize the trap. It recognized it perfectly, announced its recognition, and drank anyway. This is not a story about ignorance defeated by cleverness — it is a story about knowledge that cannot prevent its own defeat.

The Xingxing knows the past. It knows that merchants have used wine-and-sandals before. It knows that Xingxing have been caught this way. It has all the historical data.

What it lacks is the future. It cannot project from *"this has been a trap before"* to *"this specific bottle is a trap now."* The past is a pattern; the present is an instance of the pattern; the future is what happens to this specific instance. The gap between pattern and instance is where the merchants live.

There is a philosophical reading that the Tang commentators did not make explicit but that the story implies: knowledge of what has happened does not protect you from what is about to happen. History is not prophecy. Knowing what traps have looked like is not the same as knowing which objects in front of you are traps.

## After the Wine

Once the Xingxing drinks, it puts on the sandals — another element of the trap, since the sandals fit it (they are human sandals, and the Xingxing is human-sized and bipedal). With the wine inside it and the sandals slowing it down, the merchants emerge from hiding.

The Xingxing is captured. It called their names from the forest with perfect accuracy. It told them their own plan aloud. It was caught anyway.

The *Book of Rites* said it speaks but remains a beast. The folk tradition answers: it speaks better than most beasts, knows more than most humans, and is still caught by a bottle of wine left by a path. The boundary between human and animal, drawn at language, turns out to run somewhere else.
