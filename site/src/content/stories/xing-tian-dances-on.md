## The Whole Story in Twenty-Three Characters

The *Classic of Mountains and Seas*, in the *Classic of Regions Beyond the Seas: West*, gives Xing Tian (刑天) his entire biography in one breath:

> 刑天与帝至此争神，帝断其首，葬之常羊之山。乃以乳为目，以脐为口，操干戚以舞。
>
> "Xing Tian contested the godhood with the supreme god, and came to this: the god cut off his head and buried it on Mount Changyang. Whereupon Xing Tian made eyes of his nipples, made a mouth of his navel, took up his shield and axe — and danced."

That is all. The text does not say what the quarrel was about, only that it was 争神 — a contest over godhead itself, the highest stake the mythology offers. He lost. The head went into Mount Changyang. And the body declined to stop.

## The Dance

What the passage refuses to do is call it a tragedy. 操干戚以舞 — he takes up shield and axe *and dances*. The verb is dance, not rage, not haunt, not wander. Something in the old text insists that what the headless thing on the battlefield is doing is a performance: defiance done as art, the weapon-drill continued past the point of death into pure form. His name has been read as part of the sentence — 刑天, "he whom heaven punished," "the mutilated of heaven" — a being named for what was done to him, who answered by becoming what he did next.

The nipples that see and the navel that speaks are not monstrous decoration. They are the myth's logic: the seat of identity was never in the head. The god aimed at the wrong part. You cannot behead a will.

## The Poet Who Understood Him

Eight centuries later, Tao Yuanming — the farmer-poet who walked away from office to chrysanthemums and poverty — sat up at night reading the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* and wrote the reading note that made Xing Tian immortal. The tenth of his thirteen poems on the Classic runs:

> 精卫衔微木，将以填沧海。刑天舞干戚，猛志固常在。
>
> "Jingwei carries a twig in her beak, meaning to fill the grey-green sea. Xing Tian dances with shield and axe — his fierce will has always been there."

The pairing is exact: [the bird who fills the ocean](/stories/jingwei-fills-the-sea/) and the headless dancer, two beings defined by tasks that cannot be completed. Tao Yuanming's point is that completion was never the measure. 猛志固常在 — the fierce will was there before the defeat, survives it, and does not consult the outcome. Chinese readers have carried those two lines ever since as the language's shortest argument for continuing: the proof that a thing can be ended and not be finished.
