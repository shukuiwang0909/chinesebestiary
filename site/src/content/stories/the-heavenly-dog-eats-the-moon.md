## Three Animals, One Name

The tiangou (天狗) lives three lives at once, and a good story about it has to say so.

In the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*, it is an animal: a wildcat-thing with a white head, found on Yin Mountain, which wards off evil influences — the entry in the bestiary's [Tiangou page](/creatures/tiangou/). In the *Records of the Grand Historian*, the Treatise on the Heavenly Offices, it is an omen in the sky:

> 天狗，状如大奔星，有声，其下止地，类狗。所堕及，望之如火光炎炎冲天。
>
> "The Heavenly Dog has the form of a great shooting star, with sound; where it comes down to earth it resembles a dog. Wherever it falls, you see a fiery glare rising to the heavens."

A meteor, then, and a bad one — the treatise ties its fall to war and the death of commanders. The classic's ward-beast and the historian's war-star share nothing but the name and the sky.

## The Dog at the Moon

Then there is the third tiangou, the folk one, and its story is still happening. When the moon begins to dim — a bite out of the edge, the red shadow climbing — the village knows what it is seeing: the Heavenly Dog has caught the moon and is eating it. In the full folk telling, the dog is a hound of heaven let off its leash, greedy and enormous, and the moon is a cake, a pearl, a silver dish it means to swallow whole.

So the village fights back, on the sound principle that a dog eating something too fast can be startled into dropping it. Gongs, drums, pots, basins, firecrackers where they have them — everyone bangs everything at once, an entire village screaming at the sky in percussion. The dog startles. The moon slips from its jaws. The light comes back.

It always works. It has never once failed. The moon has been recovered by this method at every eclipse in living memory, and the old people will tell you so with a straight face, because it is true: bang the pots, and in an hour the moon is whole again.

## What the Noise Is For

No one in the village, by the twentieth century, believed a dog was chewing the moon. They banged the pots anyway. The story's real cargo is the moment: an eclipse is the night the familiar sky goes wrong, and the folk tale gives every household a job to do about it. A myth that converts cosmic terror into a village drumline is not ignorance; it is engineering. The astronomers wrote down the omen for the emperor. The villagers wrote down the remedy for themselves — in noise, passed hand to hand, pot to pot, from the Han to now.
