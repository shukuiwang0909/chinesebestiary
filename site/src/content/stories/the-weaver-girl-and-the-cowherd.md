## The Stars That Look Busy but Accomplish Nothing

The *Book of Songs* is not a mythology text. It is an anthology of Zhou dynasty poems — ritual hymns, love songs, political complaints — collected between roughly 1000 and 600 BCE. Somewhere in that span, a court poet in the Zhou state wrote a satire about government officials who have grand titles but accomplish nothing. For their metaphor, they used two constellations:

> 跂彼织女，终日七襄。虽则七襄，不成报章。睆彼牵牛，不以服箱。
>
> "The Weaving Girl stands on tiptoe, working her loom seven times by day. Though she works the loom seven times, she cannot weave a finished pattern. Bright is the Ox Boy, but he does not draw a carriage."

This is Zhi Nu's oldest surviving appearance in Chinese literature. She is a star that looks like it is working — she sets up her loom, she goes through the motions — but produces no cloth. The Cowherd star is there with his ox, but the ox pulls no cart. They are the appearance of function without its substance: perfect metaphors for officials who have the titles but do nothing.

There is no love story here. The stars are astronomical satire, not separated lovers. The Milky Way does not appear. There is no bridge. That came later.

## The Transformation

Between the Western Zhou and the Han dynasty, something changed. The *Nineteen Ancient Poems* (古诗十九首) — a collection of anonymous verse probably composed in the 1st or 2nd century CE — contains what many scholars consider the most influential single poem in the classical Chinese tradition on the subject of separation. Its first eight lines have been memorized by schoolchildren for two thousand years:

> 迢迢牵牛星，皎皎河汉女。
> 纤纤擢素手，札札弄机杼。
> 终日不成章，泣涕零如雨。
> 河汉清且浅，相去复几许。
> 盈盈一水间，脉脉不得语。
>
> "Far away, the Cowherd Star; bright and clear, the Girl of the Han River. Slender, slender, she lifts her white hands, click-click she plies the loom. All day she cannot finish a pattern; her tears fall like rain. The Han River is clear and shallow — how far apart are they really? Separated by just one river, gazing at each other, unable to speak."

The transformation is complete. The political satire has become a love poem. She weaves but cannot finish, not because she is a lazy official but because she cannot stop looking across the Milky Way. The tears fall like rain. The distance is both immense (the Milky Way, called here 河汉, the Han River of Heaven) and heartbreakingly small — she can see him. The separation is total and the proximity is tantalizingly real.

## The Story Beneath the Stars

By the time the Sui and Tang dynasties synthesized the full narrative, the story ran as follows. Zhi Nu was the granddaughter of the Jade Emperor (or, in earlier versions, a weaver-girl in the celestial court). The Cowherd (牛郎, Niulang) was a mortal farmer — a poor young man whose only companion was a divine ox who advised him on how to woo the goddess who had come down to bathe in the river.

They married. They were happy. They neglected everything — she stopped weaving the cloud-cloth of heaven; he stopped farming. The Jade Emperor's wife (or the Jade Emperor himself, depending on the version) drew the Milky Way across the sky between them. No crossing.

But their love moved heaven by degrees. The outcome was the minimum possible concession: one meeting per year, on the seventh night of the seventh lunar month, when all the magpies of the world fly to heaven and form a bridge (鹊桥, the Magpie Bridge) across the Milky Way. The lovers cross to each other. They have one night. At dawn the magpies return to earth and the river of stars seals them apart again.

The story is one of the few in Chinese mythology where the separation is not permanent. They do meet. The cosmos allows it — barely, once, briefly.

## Qixi and Tanabata

The Qixi Festival (七夕节, Night of Sevens) falls on the seventh night of the seventh lunar month, the night of the annual reunion. Traditionally, women demonstrated weaving and needlework skills in honor of the Weaving Girl's craft; young couples made wishes; the sky was watched for the two stars appearing close together. Rain on Qixi night was said to be Zhi Nu's tears.

In Japan, the same story — brought over in the Nara period — became Tanabata (七夕), celebrated on July 7th. Paper wishes (tanzaku) are written and hung on bamboo trees. Tanabata is one of Japan's most widely attended summer festivals and has become internationally recognizable through its presence in anime, games, and Japanese media. The Weaving Girl myth, through Japan, circulates globally in a way that most Chinese mythological stories do not.

The star Vega is directly overhead on summer evenings in the Northern Hemisphere. On a clear night, you can find Altair nearby, and the Milky Way running between them. The myth is in the sky, as it has been since the Zhou dynasty astronomers first named the stars. The bridge is not visible. But the separation is.
