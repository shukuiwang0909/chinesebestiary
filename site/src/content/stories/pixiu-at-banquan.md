## The First War Beast

The pixiu's first appearance in serious history is as a soldier. The *Records of the Grand Historian* (史记, c. 90 BCE), in its account of the Yellow Emperor's war for the world, says:

> 教熊罴貔貅貙虎，以与炎帝战于阪泉之野。
>
> "He trained the bears, the pixiu, the chu-tigers, and the tigers, and fought the Flame Emperor in the wilderness of Banquan."

The reading has been argued ever since: were these real animals drilled for war, totem standards of allied clans, or the battle-names of regiments — an armored division wearing its beast on its banner? Sima Qian does not say, and the ambiguity is the point. Three battles at Banquan broke the Flame Emperor; the alliance that won became the Chinese people, who still call themselves the heirs of Yan and Huang (炎黄子孙). Somewhere in that founding violence, on the winning side, marched the pixiu — the beast that swallows armies.

The *Book of Documents* tradition and later commentators keep the beast soldierly: the pixiu is the brave vanguard, the charge that does not stop. For most of two thousand years, that is what the name meant — a war animal, the kind of creature you want carved on a general's tomb-guard.

## The Banquet and the Seal

The second story is later, earthier, and much more famous. It is folk etiology — a just-so story for a statue — and the honest label for it is exactly that. In the folk telling, the pixiu is a young dragon-kin, the ninth and greediest of the dragon's sons, who eats nothing but treasure: gold, silver, pearls, jade. At a banquet of the Jade Emperor, the pixiu ate so greedily — and, in the crudest and most popular version, soiled the heavenly court itself — that the Emperor lost his temper and slapped it, sealing its rear passage shut.

The result is the logic every shopkeeper in Asia knows: the pixiu takes in wealth and cannot let it out. 只进不出 — "in, not out." A war beast became a vault.

The folk tradition then gets practical. A pixiu bracelet should face outward, toward the money coming in; a pixiu statue flanks the door of the bank, the casino, the gold shop; the beast should be fed (a touch of water, a stroke along its back — never its mouth, never its sealed end). Macau's casinos line them up like ushers; Hong Kong's gold shops do not open without one.

## Two Stories, One Animal

What is worth noticing is the handoff. The classical beast — the charge at Banquan, the breaker of lines — is all output: unstoppable force going outward. The folk beast is all input: treasure flowing in, nothing escaping. Same animal, opposite physics. The classical texts made the pixiu what you send; the marketplace made it what you keep. Between the Yellow Emperor's beast army and the bank manager's desk statue runs the whole arc of Chinese civilization's attention — from the battlefield to the till.
