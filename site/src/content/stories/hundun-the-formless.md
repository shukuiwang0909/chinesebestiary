## The Story in Full

The story appears in the final section of the *Zhuangzi*'s *Inner Chapters*, in a chapter titled "Responses for Emperors and Kings" (应帝王). It is the last story in the chapter and the last story in the *Inner Chapters* as a whole. Zhuangzi leaves it there without comment. Here is the complete text:

> 南海之帝为儵，北海之帝为忽，中央之帝为浑沌。儵与忽时相与遇于浑沌之地，浑沌待之甚善。儵与忽谋报浑沌之德，曰：「人皆有七窍以视听食息，此独无有，尝试凿之。」日凿一窍，七日而浑沌死。
>
> "The emperor of the South Sea was called Shu (Flash); the emperor of the North Sea was called Hu (Sudden); and the emperor of the Central Region was called Hundun (Chaos). Shu and Hu frequently met in the land of Hundun, and Hundun treated them very generously. Shu and Hu discussed how to repay Hundun's virtue: 'All men have seven openings so they can see, hear, eat, and breathe. This one alone has none. Let us try boring them for him.' So each day they bored one hole, and on the seventh day Hundun died."

That is the entire story. Forty-four Chinese characters. One of the most discussed passages in classical Chinese philosophy.

## What the Names Mean

The names are not incidental. *Shu* (儵) means something like "quick" or "flash." *Hu* (忽) means "sudden." Shu and Hu are the emperors of speed and suddenness — the imperial logic of *doing*, of *acting*, of *making change*. Their names together can be read as *suhu* (倏忽), a compound that in classical Chinese means "in an instant," the blink of an eye, the speed of decision.

*Hundun* (浑沌 or 混沌) is the word for primordial chaos, the undifferentiated state before creation split the world into categories. In cosmological terms, hundun is what existed before yin and yang, before heaven and earth separated, before names were applied to things. It is the original whole.

The meeting place is *Hundun's territory* — they are guests in chaos, and chaos has been generous to them.

## The Killing

The generosity of the two emperors is the murder weapon. They want to *improve* Hundun by giving him what every person has. The logic is impeccable by human standards: seven openings let humans interact with the world. To give Hundun seven openings is to give him the capacity to participate.

What it actually gives him is *differentiation*. The seven holes divide Hundun into a functional being — a being with specific organs for specific purposes, a being that has become a particular kind of thing rather than an undifferentiated whole. Once Hundun has eyes, he is a being that sees and therefore a being that has a particular orientation toward the visible. Once he has a mouth, he must eat, and therefore he is hungry and full, satisfied and wanting. Each hole is a definition. Each definition is a constraint.

On the seventh day, the project is complete. Hundun, who had no properties, now has seven specific properties. He is no longer whole. He dies.

## Why This Is the Last Story

The *Inner Chapters* of the *Zhuangzi* are the earliest and most carefully structured portion of the text, probably written by Zhuangzi himself. They end with this story deliberately. The final chapter, "Responses for Emperors and Kings," is about the dangers of deliberate governance — the assumption that rulers can improve their territory through action, differentiation, and the imposition of categories.

Hundun's death is the death of the uncarved block, the death of *wu wei* (non-action), the death of the state that preceded all human projects. Shu and Hu do not kill Hundun out of malice. They kill him out of the very kindness and purposefulness that makes them effective rulers — and that is Zhuangzi's point. The will to improve, to give others the tools they lack, to repay generosity with gifts: this is what kills what should not be touched.

## Hundun in the Shanhaijing

The *Shanhaijing*'s Hundun is different: a creature of the western wilderness, a red beast shaped like a bag, with no face, six legs and four wings, resembling a yellow sack of meat. This is not Zhuangzi's abstract void but a particular, locatable entity in a landscape. The two traditions use the same name for related but distinct things — the *Shanhaijing*'s Hundun is one of the Four Perils, a dangerous creature to be avoided; Zhuangzi's Hundun is the metaphysical ground of all existence.

Both traditions agree on the central attribute: Hundun has no openings, no differentiation, no specific structure. What makes it dangerous in one tradition and precious in the other is the difference between seeing chaos as threat or as source.
