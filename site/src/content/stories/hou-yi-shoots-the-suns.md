## The Parents of the Suns

Before the crisis, there was a system. The *Classic of the Great Western Wilderness* records it clearly:

> 帝俊妻羲和，生十日，方浴日于甘渊。
>
> "Di Jun's wife Xi He gave birth to the ten suns. She bathed them at the Sweet Abyss."

Each morning Xi He would prepare one of her sons for the crossing, draw him up in her sun-chariot from the Sweet Abyss of the east, and drive him across the sky in the turning nine-dragon chariot. The journey took the whole day. The other nine sons waited in the branches of the Fusang tree — the vast mulberry that stood at the world's eastern edge, its roots in the sea and its crown touching the sky.

The system assumed the sons would cooperate. They didn't.

## When All Ten Appeared

The *Huainanzi*, compiled c. 139 BCE, records the catastrophe with characteristic precision:

> 逮至尧之时，十日并出，焦禾稼，杀草木，而民无所食。猰貐、凿齿、九婴、大风、封豨、修蛇皆为民害。
>
> "In the time of Yao, ten suns appeared simultaneously. They scorched the grain and crops, killed the grasses and trees, and the people had nothing to eat. The Yayu, Chisel-Tooth, Nine Spirits, Great Wind, Giant Boar, and Long Serpent all brought harm to the people."

Two disasters arrived together: the ten suns in the sky burning everything from above, and six world-threatening monsters operating on the earth below. The six monsters are listed by name. The Long Serpent (修蛇) at the bottom of the list is the creature the *Classic of Seas Within the South* knows as the Ba She — the four-colored serpent of Dongting that swallowed elephants.

Yao, the sage emperor, was helpless. His grain was ash. His people were starving. The six monsters ranged freely. He needed someone who operated outside ordinary human capacity.

## The Commission

Di Jun acted. The *Classic of the Great Southern Wilderness* records the delegation:

> 帝俊赐羿彤弓素矰，以扶下国，羿是始去恤下地之百艰。
>
> "Di Jun gave Yi a red bow and white-fletched arrows, to support the lower world. Yi went down to relieve the ten thousand hardships of the earth."

The gift is specific: a 彤弓 (tóng gōng), a vermilion bow — the same kind of bow awarded to great military commanders in the Zhou dynasty ritual system, a mark of highest authority. Di Jun was not asking. He was commissioning.

What he was commissioning was the death of nine of his own children. The text does not dwell on this. Di Jun sends Yi. Yi goes.

## The Shooting

The shooting is brief in the oldest sources. Qu Yuan's *Questions to Heaven* (c. 4th century BCE) asks, as it asks everything:

> 羿焉彃日？乌焉解羽？
>
> "Where did Yi shoot through the suns? Where did the crow shed its feathers?"

The crow in question is the three-legged golden crow (三足乌) — the solar bird that lived inside each sun, one per disc. When Yi shot, the crow fell, and the feathers scattered. Nine crows fell. Nine suns went dark. One sun remained — the one that still crosses the sky each day, its crow intact.

The *Huainanzi* adds the detail that it was Yao who stopped Yi from shooting the last one, grabbing his arm when nine had fallen. Without that intervention, Yi would have extinguished all light.

## Six Terrors Cleared

The sky was fixed. The earth was not. Yi moved through the world, and the *Huainanzi* records his campaign:

He killed the Nine Spirits (九婴) at the Xiong River — a creature that breathed both fire and water simultaneously, producing floods and burning simultaneously.

He killed the Great Wind (大风) at Lake Qingqiu — a giant roc-like creature whose wingbeats capsized boats.

He shot the Chisel-Tooth (凿齿) at the swamps of Shouchuan — a creature with protruding blade-teeth that attacked livestock and people.

He hunted down the Giant Boar (封豨) in the mulberry grove forests — a vast wild boar that had devastated farmland across several regions.

And at Dongting Lake — the great lake of what is now Hunan province — he severed the Ba She (巴蛇), the Long Serpent that swallowed elephants whole and took three years to digest them:

> 断修蛇于洞庭。
>
> "He severed the Long Serpent at Dongting."

断 — to cut, to break, to sever. The Ba She was not stabbed or shot. Yi dismembered it at the body of water that had been its territory. The serpent's digested bones would become medicine; its territory became Lake Dongting.

## Di Jun's Price

When Yi returned, the calculation became visible. Nine suns shot down. Nine of Di Jun's sons were dead, their crows fallen from the sky with their feathers scattered. The world had been saved. The ten-sun crisis was over.

Di Jun stripped Yi of his divinity.

The *Huainanzi*'s other chapter supplies what the shooting chapter omits: Yi had been a divine archer of the celestial realm. After his campaign, he was left on earth — mortal, exiled from the divine court, stripped of whatever immortal status he had carried down with him. The text is matter-of-fact about this. Di Jun chose to save the world. He then made the person responsible for the choice pay for it.

What happened next — Yi's relationship with Chang'e, the immortality pill, the jade rabbit on the moon — belongs to a different reckoning. The world-clearing was complete. The price was settled. Yi remained on earth, the greatest archer in history, with no way back to heaven and no sun he had not shot to prove it.
