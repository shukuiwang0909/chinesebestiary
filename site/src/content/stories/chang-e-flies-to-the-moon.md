## The Pill That Existed Once

The Queen Mother of the West (西王母) kept the elixir of immortality — but kept it carefully. The *Huainanzi*'s cosmological chapters describe it in passing: Yi, having shot the nine suns and cleared the six world-threatening monsters, climbed to the Kunlun mountains and requested the pill. The Queen Mother gave him one.

One pill. The text does not say she had more. It says she gave him 不死之药 — medicine of not-dying — and implies, without stating, that the supply was extremely limited.

Yi brought it home and did not take it. The *Huainanzi* records only the bare sequence without explaining the delay: perhaps he was waiting for a ritual moment; perhaps he wanted to share it with Chang'e; perhaps he simply hadn't decided. The pill sat in the house.

## The Taking

The *Huainanzi* preserves the oldest surviving account:

> 羿请不死之药于西王母，姮娥窃以奔月。
>
> "Yi requested the medicine of not-dying from the Queen Mother of the West. Heng E stole it and fled to the moon."

That is the entire account in that text. 窃 (qiè) — stole. 奔 (bēn) — fled, ran toward. No explanation of motive, no dialogue, no moment of decision. Heng E (恒娥, later written Chang'e 嫦娥 to avoid a naming taboo on Emperor Wen's given name) took the pill and immediately ran toward the moon. The text moves past this without pause, as though the event explains itself.

It does not, quite, explain itself. The tradition that grew up around this bare sentence supplies multiple motives — depending on the source and the era:

In some versions, Yi's disciple Feng Meng had come to steal the pill by force, and Chang'e swallowed it to prevent him from having it. In others, she simply wanted immortality for herself. In others still, she was already angry at Yi for some other cause — the shooting of the suns had stripped him of divinity and left them both mortal on earth, and perhaps she did not intend to be mortal with him forever.

The *Huainanzi* records none of this. It records only that she stole it and ran.

## What She Became

The *Guizang* — an ancient divination text known now only through citations, attributed to a pre-Zhou tradition — preserves what is probably the oldest version of Chang'e's fate on the moon:

> 昔嫦娥以西王母不死之药服之，遂奔月为月精。
>
> "Formerly Chang'e took the Queen Mother of the West's medicine of not-dying and swallowed it; she then fled to the moon and became the essence of the moon."

Other early sources are less elevated: Chang'e became a toad. The moon toad (月蟾) — the three-legged toad visible in the dark patches of the moon's face — is identified in multiple Han dynasty sources as Chang'e transformed. The *Lingxian* (灵宪, by Zhang Heng, c. 100 CE) says she swallowed the pill and "became a toad" before specifying she is the "essence of the moon."

Han dynasty tomb art from Mawangdui and elsewhere depicts the moon as containing both a toad and a jade rabbit together — both associated with Chang'e's presence there. The rabbit grinds the immortality ingredients; the toad is what she was, or became, or carries.

## Alone in the Cold Palace

The dominant tradition in Chinese poetry does not dwell on the toad. It dwells on the cold.

The moon palace (月宫, also called the Cold Palace — 广寒宫, literally the Palace of Wide Coldness) is not comfortable. It is beautiful and frozen. Chang'e is its only permanent resident. She has the jade rabbit, who pounds the mortar eternally. She has the cassia tree, which a man named Wu Gang is perpetually ordered to chop down (it grows back every stroke). She has immortality.

What she does not have is warmth, or Yi, or the earth.

The Tang dynasty poet Li Bai writes of her "regret":

> 嫦娥应悔偷灵药，碧海青天夜夜心。
>
> "Chang'e must regret stealing the divine medicine: the blue sea and the azure sky — her heart, night after night."

This reading — Chang'e as a figure of permanent regret — is dominant in the poetic tradition. Whether she actually regrets it, the ancient texts do not say. The *Huainanzi* records only that she ran. Whether running was right or wrong, she cannot undo it.

## The Myth That Stays

The Mid-Autumn Festival (中秋节) falls on the fifteenth of the eighth lunar month — the night of the year's fullest moon. It is one of the most widely celebrated festivals in the Chinese-speaking world, and its mythological center is Chang'e: mooncakes are eaten, the moon is watched, and the dark shape in the lunar surface is understood as the silhouette of a woman and a rabbit in an endless moonlit room.

The story's grip is not difficult to understand. It is a myth about an irreversible decision — the kind you cannot take back because you already swallowed it. The immortality was real. The loneliness was the price. Chang'e is on the moon forever, which is exactly what she wanted and exactly what she cannot undo.
