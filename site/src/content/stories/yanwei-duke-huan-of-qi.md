## Two Texts, Two Omens

The *Shanhaijing* and the *Zhuangzi* describe the same creature and reach opposite conclusions.

The *Classic of Regions Within the Seas* says:

> 人主得而飨食之，伯天下。
>
> "If a ruler obtains it and hosts and feeds it, he will become hegemon over all the world."

The *Zhuangzi*'s "Mastering Life" says:

> 见则其邑大饥。
>
> "Seeing it means great famine for that settlement."

Hegemony. Famine. The same double-headed serpent in purple robes, as long as a carriage shaft, with a human head at each end. Two texts in the same tradition, a century or two apart, have looked at this creature and filed it under completely different categories of fate.

The *Zhuangzi* has a story. The *Shanhaijing* has only a fact. The story helps explain the discrepancy.

## The Hunt at the Marsh

Duke Huan of Qi was the first of the Spring and Autumn period's Five Hegemons — the most powerful lord of his era, a man who made and unmade alliances between the great states, presided over interstate ritual, and held a position that made him, in all but name, the effective ruler of the Chinese world. He had the minister Guan Zhong, who is considered the greatest statesman in Chinese political history, a man who built Qi's military and commercial power from structural reforms. They were a partnership that defined an era.

The *Zhuangzi* puts Duke Huan at a marsh, hunting. His companions return. He goes out alone into the reeds.

He sees something.

He comes home. He does not say what he saw. He takes to bed with an illness that has no obvious cause. He is sick for several days. His ministers and physicians find nothing wrong with him. He remains ill.

Guan Zhong comes to see him.

## What Guan Zhong Knew

"Your Highness," Guan Zhong asks, "did you see something during the hunt?"

Duke Huan — perhaps relieved to be asked — admits that he did. At the marsh, in the reeds, he saw a creature. He describes it: large, not a snake exactly, but snake-bodied. Two heads. Purple robes. A cap.

Guan Zhong names it without hesitation.

> 委蛇，其大如毂，其长如辕，紫衣而朱冠。
>
> "The Weiyi — as wide as a wheel hub, as long as a carriage shaft, purple robes and a red cap."

Guan Zhong explains that the Weiyi lives in marshes, that it fears the sound of thunder (when it hears thunder, both heads rise simultaneously), that seeing it portends famine for the settlement where it appears.

The *Zhuangzi* records what happens next: Duke Huan laughs, says his spirits are lifted, gets up from bed, and recovers.

The illness was fright. The treatment was explanation. He saw something he did not have a category for — a snake with two heads in court robes, something that should not exist — and the wrongness of it made him ill. Guan Zhong gave him a category. The Weiyi is a known creature. It has a name. It has a description. It has an omen. Once the formless wrongness has a name, it becomes manageable; once manageable, it loses its power to terrify.

## The Hegemony Version

The *Shanhaijing*'s account does not include Duke Huan. It simply states the fact: a ruler who hosts the Yanwei at a feast will become hegemon over all the world.

Guo Pu, the fourth-century commentator, identified the Yanwei and the Weiyi as the same creature. If he is right, then the same serpent gave Duke Huan an illness that his minister had to cure — and in the *Shanhaijing* version would have given him hegemony over the world if only he had invited it to dinner instead of running.

The discrepancy between famine and hegemony becomes, in this reading, a story about readiness. Duke Huan saw the Yanwei and panicked. A ruler who was *ready* — who saw the creature, recognized its nature, and extended formal hospitality — would receive the hegemony gift. Duke Huan was already a hegemon; perhaps that is why he encountered the creature and received its lesser gift (fright cured by explanation) rather than its greater one.

The *Zhuangzi*'s famine omen fits the accidental encounter. The *Shanhaijing*'s hegemony gift fits the intentional one. The creature does not change; the quality of the encounter changes what flows from it.

## The Double Head

The *Zhuangzi*'s story does not remark on the double-headed anatomy. Guan Zhong describes the creature — its size, its robes — but does not theorize about what two heads mean. He is a statesman and a pragmatist; he gives Duke Huan enough information to stop being afraid, not a lecture on cosmological symbolism.

The symbolism is there anyway. A creature with a head at each end cannot be said to have a direction. It cannot be said to be coming or going. It occupies both positions at once — arrival and departure, past and future, this end and that end — and those positions are identical. Yanwei is the creature that the world's division into opposite ends has not fully reached. It is, in the philosophical vocabulary that the *Zhuangzi* would have recognized, adjacent to Hundun — the undifferentiated primordial chaos.

The *Zhuangzi* does not make this connection explicitly. But it is the book that most consistently asks: what do we do when the categories fail? What do we do when we see something that should not exist?

Duke Huan saw the answer: we get sick, and we wait for someone to give it a name.
