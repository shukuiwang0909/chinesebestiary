## The War Before Memory

The Shanhaijing and the Huainanzi agree on one thing about Gonggong: he was always fighting. The texts record at least two great conflicts — one against Zhurong, the fire god, and one against Zhuanxu, the heaven-sovereign. The sources disagree about the opponent, the cause, and the outcome. What they agree on is what Gonggong did when he lost.

The Huainanzi (淮南子), compiled in the second century BCE under Liu An, Prince of Huainan, gives the fullest account:

> 昔者共工与颛顼争为帝，怒而触不周之山，天柱折，地维绝。天倾西北，故日月星辰移焉；地不满东南，故水潦尘埃归焉。
>
> "In ancient times, Gonggong contended with Zhuanxu for the rulership of heaven. In fury, he rammed Mount Buzhou. The pillar of heaven broke; the cords of earth snapped. Heaven tilted toward the northwest — which is why the sun, moon, and stars all move in that direction. Earth became hollow in the southeast — which is why all water, floods, and dust gather there."

This is Chinese cosmological geology. The reason the sky is higher in the northwest and the rivers run east — the literal, observable facts of Chinese geography — is Gonggong's temper. The world as it exists is the scar of a god's failure.

## What Mount Buzhou Was

Before Gonggong, Mount Buzhou (不周山 — "Mountain of Incompleteness") stood at the northwest corner of the world as one of the pillars holding the sky aloft. Chinese cosmology of this era imagined the sky as a dome resting on mountains at the cardinal corners, with the earth below slightly bowl-shaped. The cords connecting sky and earth reinforced the structure. Gonggong's impact knocked out the supporting pillar and snapped the cords.

Qu Yuan's *Questions to Heaven* (天问, c. 4th century BCE) asks the question almost philosophically:

> 康回冯怒，墬何故以东南倾？
>
> "Kanghui (Gonggong's alternate name) raged in fury — why did the earth tilt toward the southeast?"

The poem asks but does not answer, treating the geological fact as a divine riddle. Seven centuries later the Huainanzi gives the answer in full.

## The Void That Nuwa Saw

What Gonggong left behind was not merely a broken mountain but a hole in heaven — a gap in the northwest sky through which something leaked, or nothing held. The Huainanzi describes what happened next in a passage that has no exact parallel in world mythology:

> 往古之时，四极废，九州裂，天不兼覆，地不周载。火爁焱而不灭，水浩洋而不息。猛兽食颛民，鸷鸟攫老弱。于是女娲炼五色石以补苍天，断鳌足以立四极，杀黑龙以济冀州，积芦灰以止淫水。
>
> "In the ancient past, the four poles were ruined, the nine provinces split open, heaven no longer covered everything, earth no longer bore everything up. Fire blazed without ceasing; the waters surged without stopping. Ferocious animals devoured the people; birds of prey seized the old and the weak. Then Nuwa smelted five-colored stones to patch the azure sky, severed the legs of the great turtle to set up the four poles, slew the black dragon to rescue Ji Province, and piled up reed ash to stop the flooding waters."

The scale of the repair required matches the scale of the damage. Nuwa does not simply fill the hole — she reconstructs the entire cosmological infrastructure: new poles, new framework, a flood suppressed. The five-colored stones she smelts remain an image that commentators have never exhausted: what do the five colors mean? (The five elements. The five directions. Or simply the complexity of what it takes to restore what was broken.) One stone is left over — a raw unmelted remainder, the material of all the world's creative surplus, which later mythology will give its own destiny.

## What the Tilting Made

The world Gonggong made — tilted, southeast-draining — is the world China actually inhabits. The Yellow River, the Yangtze, and every other major Chinese river flow eastward into the sea. The northwestern reaches are high plateau and desert. The sky dome, observable in the arc of the Milky Way, does seem to tilt. Whether or not you believe Gonggong rammed its support, the story fits the landscape with the satisfaction of a well-matched explanation.

What it says about failure is harder to resolve. Gonggong did not intend to tilt the world. He intended to destroy his enemy's victory. The collateral damage was everything. In Chinese tradition, this is neither celebrated nor condemned but simply recorded: a god's rage, uncontained, became the shape of the world. The water still runs southeast.
