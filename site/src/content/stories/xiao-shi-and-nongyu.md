## The Original Passage

The *Biographies of Immortals* (列仙传), the Han-dynasty register of transcendents, keeps the whole story in a few lines:

> 箫史者，秦穆公时人也。善吹箫，能致孔雀、白鹤于庭。穆公有女，字弄玉，好之。公遂以女妻焉。日教弄玉作凤鸣。居数年，吹似凤声，凤凰来止其屋。公为作凤台。夫妇止其上，不下数年。一旦，皆乘凤皇飞去。
>
> "Xiao Shi lived in the time of Duke Mu of Qin. He played the xiao flute so well that peacocks and white cranes gathered in his courtyard. The Duke had a daughter, styled Nongyu, who loved his playing; the Duke gave her to him in marriage. Day by day he taught Nongyu to make the phoenix's cry. After some years her playing was like the phoenix's own voice, and phoenixes came and perched upon their house. The Duke built them a Phoenix Terrace, and the couple dwelt upon it, not descending for years. Then one morning they both mounted phoenixes and flew away."

That is the entire text — and it has been a love story for two thousand years.

## The Flute

The xiao (箫) is the vertical bamboo flute — the quietest instrument in the Chinese orchestra, breathy, close-miked, the sound of one person alone with evening. Xiao Shi's name is just "the xiao player"; the text gives him no other. What the story insists on is not his lineage but his competence: his playing does not command the birds, it *interests* them. Peacocks and cranes in the courtyard are the first review. The phoenixes come later, and only when the duet is complete.

The detail worth dwelling on is the pedagogy. He does not play *at* Nongyu; he teaches her to make the phoenix cry herself. Years of it. The birds arrive only when there are two players, when the imitation is mutual — the phoenix answering what sounds, by then, very much like another phoenix. The story is careful about this: 吹似凤声, "the playing resembled the phoenix's voice." Courtship by long practice.

## The Terrace and the Departure

Duke Mu builds the Phoenix Terrace — 凤台 — for the couple, and they live on it, "not descending for years." The tower is the world's recognition: the duke acknowledging that his daughter has married out of the human order, into something with feathers. And then, one morning, the departure that Chinese poetry has never stopped quoting: 一旦，皆乘凤皇飞去 — "one day, they both mounted phoenixes and flew away."

No farewell, no grief. The text closes the door gently. Nongyu becomes the eternal figure of the woman carried out of the world by art and by love; "riding the phoenix" (乘凤) enters the language as the idiom for a daughter marrying well; the Phoenix Terrace becomes a standing poetic site. When the bestiary's [Fenghuang entry](/creatures/fenghuang/) says the bird appears where the world is at peace, this is the proof the tradition kept returning to: a duke's court so harmonious that the omen-birds came down and carried two people off.

## The Phoenix as Marriage Omen

This is why, at Chinese weddings, the fenghuang stands for the bride — not because the bird is female in the old texts (the feng was male, the huang female), but because of *this story*: the woman who learned the phoenix's voice and left the world on its back. Every phoenix embroidered on a wedding dress is, whether the wearer knows it or not, Nongyu's ride out of Qin.
