## The Case for Pixiu

The *Classic of the Western Mountains* (山海经·西山经) describes the pixiu's territory: it inhabits the extreme western mountains, it is categorized as one of the auspicious beasts, and it appears on military banners to summon brave soldiers. This creature is not ornamental. It is an active force — a divine predator that hunts, that fights, that occupies terrain that lesser creatures avoid.

The folk tradition that grew around the pixiu after the Shanhaijing adds the crucial anatomical detail: it eats gold, silver, and jewels, and it has no exit for what it consumes. Everything goes in; nothing comes out. In a fight in a treasury vault — the arena we have proposed — pixiu is not fighting for survival. It is fighting on home territory, surrounded by its food source, in conditions that fuel its power directly.

The deeper advantage is structural. Pixiu is genuinely amoral. It does not fight for good. It does not fight for evil. It fights for wealth, and it is not confused about the difference. Against Qiongqi — whose entire function depends on identifying and inverting moral polarity — pixiu presents a target with no readable moral signature. You cannot reward evil in a creature that has no alignment to good or evil. You cannot punish virtue in a creature that does not practice it.

## The Case for Qiongqi

Qiongqi's entry in the Shanhaijing is brief and unsettling. It appears as one of the Four Perils — alongside Taotie, Taowu, and Hundun — classified as the most disruptive forces in the cosmos. Its specific gift is documented: it rewards those who commit evil and attacks the loyal and honest. In Chinese mythology, the Four Perils are not simply strong creatures. They represent principles — the principles that corrode civilization from within by inverting its values.

In this fight, Qiongqi's advantage is unpredictability. It does not fight the way a divine guardian fights. It does not escalate force against force. It probes for the alignment axis — finds the angle where the opponent's virtue becomes a liability — and attacks there. Against Pixiu, Qiongqi would not need to overpower it. It would need to find the crack, the single point where Pixiu's single-minded appetite becomes a weakness.

There is an argument that pure greed is itself a moral position — that Pixiu's avarice is the kind of vice Qiongqi rewards. If Qiongqi is boosting the morally impure, and Pixiu is quite impure, then Qiongqi might be boosting the enemy. But this argument cuts in both directions.

## The Verdict

**Pixiu wins — but the match is weirder than the score suggests.** Qiongqi needs a moral axis to invert. Its power is specifically the corruption of beings that have moral alignments — loyal officials, honest citizens, righteous soldiers. Against those targets it is devastating. Against Pixiu, it is attempting to apply the gravity of moral inversion to a creature that has no gravity to pull. Pixiu does not value justice; Qiongqi cannot corrupt its sense of justice. Pixiu does not practice loyalty; Qiongqi cannot attack its loyalty. The inversion engine runs at idle.

What Qiongqi *can* do is fight physically — it has tiger stripes, fierce claws, and genuine ferocity. But Pixiu was a war beast first and a feng shui ornament second. It spent its mythological career on military banners, not in offices.

Pixiu wins by being the one opponent in Chinese mythology that Qiongqi's specific power is useless against. It is not a moral victory. That is exactly why it is a victory.
