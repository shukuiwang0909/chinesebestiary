## The Case for Qiongqi

Qiongqi is the Four Perils entry with the best résumé: a **winged tiger** that rides the storm-winds, or — in the second, irreconcilable account — a quill-armored ox whose hide turns blades. It devours people beginning at the skull, understands human speech, and, in the later gloss that makes this matchup delicious, **hunts whoever is in the right**. By that logic the Qilin is not merely a target but the target — the most righteous being in the canon, an omen so virtuous it appears only when a true king reigns.

And Qiongqi fights the way predators actually win: from above, at the moment of its choosing, against a herbivore's body plan.

## The Case for Qilin

Do not mistake gentleness for weakness. The Qilin's stats tell a different story: flesh-tipped horn (a weapon that chooses never to cut), a coat of five-colored flame, hooves that cross snow and mud without a print, and — decisively — **divine timing**. It appears only when the realm is just. An omen-beast of the Four Divine does not get ambushed in a lawless wasteland; if it stands in that clearing, the narrative itself has already sided with it. He Xiu's Han commentary defines its whole nature as "bearing arms yet harming none" — a defense so complete it has no opening for Qiongqi's brand of opportunism.

## The Verdict

**Draw — by design, and the texts back it.** Qiongqi's later-attributed power only activates against the righteous *as prey*, but the Qilin cannot be prey: harming it is the omen that ends dynasties, and no Peril in the record has ever survived being the reason a kingdom falls. Meanwhile the Qilin, sworn to harm none, has no attack vector at all — the quill-hide shrugs off the horn it would never lower anyway. Stalemate, permanently. **Verdict: the wastes stay quiet. Neither falls; neither yields.**
