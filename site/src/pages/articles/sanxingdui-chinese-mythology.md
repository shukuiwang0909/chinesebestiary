In 1986, workers deepening a drainage ditch in Guanghan, Sichuan, hit bronze. Over the next months, archaeologists excavated two ritual pits crammed with objects that fit no category in Chinese Bronze Age scholarship: bronze masks with eyes on cylindrical stalks, a standing human figure nearly two meters tall, a sacred tree over four meters high hung with jade discs and bronze birds, gold foil poured over wooden forms, elephant tusks stacked by the dozen. New pits were found in 2020 and yielded more. The site is called Sanxingdui (三星堆) — Three Star Mounds — and thirty-eight years after its discovery, it remains one of the most discussed and least resolved questions in Chinese archaeology.

The standard account of Chinese Bronze Age civilization centers on the Yellow River valley: Erlitou, Zhengzhou, Anyang, the Shang and Zhou dynasties. Sanxingdui was 1,500 kilometers to the southwest, in the Chengdu plain, making objects on a scale and with an aesthetic that shared almost nothing with the Shang bronzes that were supposed to define the era. The puzzle is not simply "who made these?" — the answer is broadly the Sanxingdui culture, an early phase of the ancient Shu kingdom. The puzzle is: what mythology were they illustrating?

## The Vertical Eyes and Cancong

The most distinctive artifacts at Sanxingdui are the bronze masks with protruding eyes. Ordinary bronze masks from the site have normal eye placements. The 纵目铜面具 (protruding-eye bronze masks) have cylindrical bronze pupils that extend forward from the face on stalks — the most dramatic example (artifact K2②：149-1) projects 16 centimeters. This is not a stylization found anywhere else in the Bronze Age world. It represents a specific, intentional choice to depict eyes that exist in a direction that human eyes do not.

One text uses almost this exact description for a divine ruler of ancient Shu. The *Chronicle of the Kings of Shu* (蜀王本纪, attributed to Yang Xiong, c. 53 BCE–18 CE) records the first king of Shu in seven characters: 蜀候蚕丛，其目纵，始称王 — "The Shu lord Cancong, his eyes vertical, was the first to be called king." No other feature is given. His reign, his deeds, his death: absent. The eyes are his entire biography.

The connection between Cancong's 纵目 and the Sanxingdui masks is the most widely discussed link between textual mythology and Bronze Age archaeology in Sichuan scholarship. It is also unconfirmed. The chronicle postdates the masks by roughly 1,000–1,200 years, and Sanxingdui left no writing. The masks may illustrate Cancong; they may illustrate a divine being whose vertical-eye iconography was later ascribed to him; the chronicle may describe a tradition that was already old and already mysterious when Yang Xiong recorded it.

What is certain is this: the vertical-eye tradition was specific to Shu. No Shang bronze, no Zhou vessel, no Central Plains artifact depicts eyes this way. The Shu kingdom — which Li Bai would later call older than memory, isolated from the Qin borderland by roads harder to travel than the road to heaven — maintained a cosmological vocabulary that the Yellow River civilization did not share.

## The Sacred Tree and the Kunlun Cosmology

If the bronze masks are the most debated artifact at Sanxingdui, the bronze sacred trees (青铜神树) are the most cosmologically significant. The largest surviving example — partially reconstructed — stands over 3.95 meters tall. It has three tiers of branches, each bearing fruits and birds. A serpentine form winds around the trunk. At the top, a further branch extension was likely present. Birds perch on the outer branches; the number and arrangement have been compared to the ten suns of the Di Jun mythology, the cosmic birds of the eastern pole.

The structure is a cosmic axis — a world tree connecting heaven and earth at a vertical point — and it parallels a tradition the *Shanhaijing* preserves in its western chapters. The *Classic of Regions Within the Seas: West* describes Kunlun Mountain at the center of the western world: eight hundred li on each side, ten thousand *ren* high, with sacred grain growing on its summit and nine wells framed in jade on each of its four faces. At each of the nine gates of Kunlun stands the Kaiming beast — a tiger-bodied guardian with nine human-faced heads, all oriented to face east — protecting the hundred gods who reside inside.

The parallel structures are striking: a cosmic center guarded by a multi-faced entity, sacred trees as cosmic axes, birds associated with the celestial order. Scholars including K.C. Chang and Robert Bagley have noted these parallels in the context of asking whether the Sanxingdui culture and the *Shanhaijing*'s western cosmology share a common mythological vocabulary. The Shu kingdom inhabited the same geography the *Shanhaijing* designates as its western quadrant — the Chengdu Basin, the Min River valley, the ranges between Sichuan and Tibet. Whether the *Shanhaijing*'s description of Kunlun was influenced by Shu traditions, or whether both independently organized the far west around the same cosmological logic, remains open.

## Taotie: A Shang Bronze and a Shu Bronze

One of the most common connections made between Sanxingdui and mainstream Chinese mythology is the taotie mask. The *taotie* face — a symmetrical, frontally depicted animal face with prominent eyes and horns, usually without a lower jaw — dominated Shang dynasty ritual bronzes from roughly 1600 BCE onward. It appears on ding, zun, and other vessel shapes in every major Shang and early Zhou site.

Sanxingdui bronzes also have faces on them. But the Sanxingdui faces are different in almost every respect: they are three-dimensional rather than surface relief; they are full faces with lower jaws, not the split-mask convention; and their aesthetic is naturalistic where the taotie mask is abstract and geometric. The protruding-eye masks have the volume and specific features of portraits, not of the ornamental taotie pattern.

The connection between Sanxingdui and taotie iconography exists at a higher level of abstraction: both cultures produced frontally depicted faces on sacred objects; both traditions associated faces with ritual protection or divine power. But the specific taotie mask convention — the bilateral symmetry, the absent lower jaw, the horn arrangements — is a Shang and Zhou Central Plains tradition that does not appear at Sanxingdui in any clear form. The more accurate claim is that the Sanxingdui face tradition and the taotie tradition are parallel developments in a Bronze Age China that was not as culturally unified as later accounts assumed.

## What Sanxingdui Changes

The discovery and continued excavation of Sanxingdui has produced several revisions to the standard picture of Chinese Bronze Age civilization:

**The Bronze Age was not a single tradition.** The Shang dynasty's cultural reach was extensive but not total. The Shu kingdom maintained a sophisticated bronze-casting tradition with its own iconographic vocabulary, its own ritual objects, and almost certainly its own mythology — a mythology that survived only in fragments in texts written a thousand years after Sanxingdui's great ritual deposits.

**Chinese mythology has a western tradition that is poorly preserved.** The texts that survive — *Shanhaijing*, *Huainanzi*, *Shu Wang Benji*, *Huayang Guozhi* — give glimpses of a western cosmological system organized around Kunlun, the Queen Mother of the West, sacred trees, and divine guardians. Sanxingdui suggests that this western tradition was not merely literary but was embodied in a living Bronze Age culture with its own monumental art.

**The "vertical eyes" tradition may be much older than its textual record.** Cancong appears in texts from the Han dynasty, but the Sanxingdui masks were made 1,000–1,200 years earlier. Either the Han texts preserve an oral tradition of extraordinary age, or the masks and the texts are independent expressions of a longer regional convention that associated divine vision with eyes that extended beyond the normal plane of the face.

**The silence of Sanxingdui is itself a datum.** Sanxingdui left no writing. The pits were sealed deliberately — everything in them was burned before burial, suggesting a ritual termination rather than a catastrophe. Whatever the Sanxingdui culture knew about its own mythology, it chose not to write down, and the tradition that continued after its decline did not preserve what came before. The masks and trees survive; the stories that explained them do not.
