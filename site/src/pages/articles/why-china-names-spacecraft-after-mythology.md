## Why China Names Its Spacecraft After Mythology

*A field guide to the celestial bureaucracy*

In 2021, a Chinese rover rolled onto the surface of Mars bearing the name of a fire god. In 2022, a satellite began measuring the carbon breath of Earth's forests under the name of a bird-bodied wood deity. China's space program is not merely inspired by its mythology — it is administered by it. Rockets, rovers, stations, and probes have been organized into a functioning celestial bureaucracy, with each machine assigned a mythological officeholder appropriate to its duties.

Western coverage tends to treat this as charming cultural branding. That misses the depth. The naming system does three jobs at once: it compresses complex missions into instantly memorable symbols, it asserts cultural continuity with an unbroken classical tradition, and — for those who care to look — it points directly back to source texts, some over two millennia old. This guide traces every major name to its origin, with the original mythology and what the machine actually does.

### The Moon Ministry: Chang'e, Yutu, and Queqiao

The lunar program is the oldest and most elaborately named branch of the celestial bureaucracy. [Chang'e](/creatures/chang-e/) is the moon goddess of the *Huainanzi*: wife of the divine archer Yi, she drank the elixir of immortality and fled to the moon, where she dwells to this day. When China's first lunar orbiter launched in 2007, naming it for her was obvious — the mission was, literally, a visit to her residence. Six missions in, the name has acquired layers of appropriateness: Chang'e-6's 2024 far-side sample return succeeded partly because the relay satellite network made the goddess's far-side home reachable.

Yutu, the Jade Rabbit, is her companion in the lunar palace: the rabbit who pounds the elixir of immortality with a pestle, visible in the moon's shadows to anyone taught to look for it. The rovers sent to keep the goddess company are, in this frame, not machines on a dead world but her long-awaited attendants.

Queqiao — "Magpie Bridge" — completes the trio with the program's most precise naming. In the legend, the Cowherd and the Weaver Girl are lovers separated by the Milky Way, permitted to meet once a year when magpies form a bridge across it. The Queqiao satellites are relay craft stationed where no direct signal can reach: they hold position between Earth and the moon's far side, forming exactly the bridge the legend describes. When Chang'e-4 made the first far-side landing in 2019, every byte of its data crossed a magpie bridge.

### The Solar Bureau: Xihe, Kuafu — and the Shanhaijing Connection

Here the naming system reaches deepest into the classical layer, because the sun deities of Chinese mythology are creatures of the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* (山海经, c. 4th century BCE) — the very text this bestiary catalogs.

[Xihe](/creatures/xi-he/) appears in the *Classic of the Great Wilderness: South*: "Xihe, wife of Di Jun, gave birth to the ten suns." The mother of the ten suns — nine of whom were shot down by the archer Yi when they rose together and scorched the earth — now lends her name to China's first dedicated solar observation satellite. Launched in October 2021, the Chinese Hα Solar Explorer watches our single remaining sun in hydrogen-alpha light, monitoring the flares and eruptions that the legend would have read as the surviving sun-child misbehaving.

[Kuafu](/creatures/kua-fu/) is the giant of the *Classic of Regions Beyond the Seas: North*, who chased the sun across the sky to catch it, ran until he died of thirst, and whose abandoned staff sprouted into a forest of peach trees. In October 2022, the Advanced Space-based Solar Observatory took his name — Kuafu-1 — for a machine that does what the giant could not: watch the sun continuously, without exhaustion, mapping its magnetic fields and coronal eruptions from orbit. The legend ends in heroic failure; the observatory is the giant's second, successful run.

For readers of this bestiary, the solar bureau's names deserve special attention: [Xihe](/creatures/xi-he/) and [Kuafu](/creatures/kua-fu/) both have full entries, original texts, and translations.

### The Ministry of Fire: Zhurong on Mars

[Zhurong](/creatures/zhurong/) is the god of fire, the south, and summer — and a genuine Shanhaijing figure, appearing in the *Classic of Regions Within the Seas* as a descendant of the Flame Emperor through six named generations. When the Tianwen-1 mission's rover needed a name, the choice was mythological engineering at its finest: Mars was Yinghuo, the "Sparkling Deluder," to China's ancient astronomers — a red, wandering star of fire and war and omens. Sending the god of fire to walk the red planet resolves an omen two thousand years in the making. The rover landed in Utopia Planitia in May 2021 and operated for over a Martian year; its resting place is now, in a sense, the south god's new domain.

### The Forest Office: Goumang Counts the World's Trees

The quietest perfect naming in the entire system. [Goumang](/creatures/goumang/) appears in the *Classic of Regions Beyond the Seas: East*: "Goumang of the East — bird's body, human face, riding two dragons." He is the god of wood, of spring, and of the eastern quarter of the world. In August 2022, the Terrestrial Ecosystem Carbon Inventory Satellite — which measures global vegetation and carbon flux, watching whether Earth's forests thrive or die — was named for him. The deity who governed the forests of the East is now an eye in the sky governing the forests of the whole Earth.

### The Infrastructure: Beidou, Tiangong, Shenzhou

Not every name is a deity. The workhorse systems draw on cosmology and state tradition.

Beidou is the Northern Dipper — the Big Dipper asterism, whose handle Chinese astronomy used as a cosmic clock and compass for millennia. The *Records of the Grand Historian* (1st c. BCE) already treats it as the pivot of celestial navigation. A satellite navigation constellation named "the Dipper" is a name with a two-thousand-year product spec behind it.

Tiangong, the Heavenly Palace, is the Daoist celestial court — the seat of the Jade Emperor and his administration, familiar to every reader of *Journey to the West*. China's orbital station names its modules in the same register: Tianhe ("Harmony of the Heavens"), Wentian ("Questioning the Heavens"), Mengtian ("Dreaming of the Heavens"). The station is not merely called a palace; its rooms are named like palace halls.

Shenzhou, the crewed spacecraft, puns on two homophones: 神舟, "divine vessel," and 神州, an ancient name for China itself, recorded in the *Records of the Grand Historian* ("the Middle Kingdom is called the Red County and Divine Land"). The crewed ship is thus simultaneously "divine vessel" and "Chinese vessel" — one syllable carrying both.

### The Fundamental Science Wing: Wukong and Mozi

The particle physicists and quantum scientists reached for different shelves.

Wukong — the Monkey King, whose dharma name means "Awakened to Emptiness" — is an almost too-appropriate patron for a dark matter detector. The Dark Matter Particle Explorer, launched in 2015, takes his name with full awareness of the pun: a satellite hunting invisible matter, named for a sage who could see through all illusions with his fiery eyes.

Mozi honors the Warring States philosopher whose *Mo Jing* contains China's earliest systematic optics — pinhole imaging, plane mirrors, the properties of light. The quantum science satellite that demonstrated satellite-based quantum key distribution carries the name of the first Chinese optical physicist, twenty-four centuries prior.

### The Fiction Frontier: The Nantianmen Project

And then there is the strangest entry in the celestial bureaucracy — one that does not yet exist.

The [Nantianmen Project](/articles/nantianmen-project/) (南天门计划, "South Gate of Heaven Project") began around 2017 as a science-fiction intellectual property of the Aviation Industry Corporation of China, the state aerospace giant. In its fictional universe, Earth faces extraterrestrial threats, and humanity responds with a fleet named entirely from mythology: the [Luan Bird](/creatures/luan-bird/) (鸾鸟) — a colossal airborne carrier of well over 100,000 tons; the [Xuannü](/creatures/jiutian-xuannu/) (玄女), an unmanned space fighter; the Baidi (白帝, "White Emperor"), a hypersonic fighter; and the Chengying (承影), a manned mech.

The Shanhaijing connection is explicit and delightful: the Luan Bird of the *Classic of the Mountains: West* is a five-colored pheasant-like omen whose appearance heralds peace under heaven ("when the Luan Bird appears, the world is at ease"). The mythical omen of peace, reimagined with hangar decks.

What elevated the Nantianmen Project from airshow curiosity to international news was ambiguity. AVIC presents it as fiction — elaborate concept art, model kits, animated shorts — while statements by state-linked figures have repeatedly blurred the line between universe-building and technology aspiration, most recently in a flurry of 2026 reports about a "confirmed" 120,000-ton orbital strike carrier. Western defense media cannot decide whether to file it under science fiction, propaganda, or roadmap. The honest classification is that it is all three at once, in proportions that remain classified even from itself.

For mythology readers, though, its meaning is simpler: the celestial bureaucracy has become self-aware. The same imagination that assigned Xihe to the sun and Zhurong to Mars is now designing, in fiction, the hardware of heaven's own gate — with the South Gate staffed, naturally, by creatures from the Shanhaijing.

### Why It Works

Strip away the nationalism and the branding analysis, and the system remains elegant on purely engineering grounds. Mythological names are:

- **Mnemonic.** "The rover named after the fire god went to the red planet" is a fact nobody forgets.
- **Compressible.** Each name carries a cosmological job description — the Dipper navigates, the Palace houses, the Bridge relays.
- **Legible across time.** The names were chosen from texts that have been continuously read for over two thousand years, which is more than can be said of most mission nomenclature.

Other spacefaring nations chose gods and heroes for their rockets — Apollo, Saturn, Soyuz's folk names, Gagarin. China's distinction is not that it mythologizes its hardware, but that it keeps the paperwork: every name comes with a source, a chapter, and an original text. This bestiary exists to hold that paperwork.
